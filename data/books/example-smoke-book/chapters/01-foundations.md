# Foundations

Structured thinking starts with the claim that complex topics become easier to understand when the author separates **observation**, **interpretation**, and **decision**. Many readers collapse these layers into one reaction, which makes later disagreement look personal rather than analytical.

The chapter defines a working model. Observation refers to what can be directly described. Interpretation refers to the meaning assigned to those descriptions. Decision refers to the action chosen after weighing competing interpretations. This model is simple, but it matters because it prevents people from acting as if every first impression is already a justified conclusion.

## Why the distinction matters

The distinction matters because the same evidence can support several plausible interpretations. For example, a decline in sales may reflect weaker demand, a change in channel mix, or a deliberate move upmarket. If the analyst jumps from the observation to a single explanation, the resulting decision may look confident while still being weakly grounded.

The chapter also introduces a qualifier. The model is useful only if the reader is willing to state uncertainty explicitly. In practice, many decisions must still be made before certainty is available, so the goal is not perfect knowledge. The goal is to preserve the difference between what is known, what is inferred, and what remains uncertain.
