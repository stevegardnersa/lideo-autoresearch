# Chapter 1
# The Summer of AI
The first obstacle to thinking clearly about AI is the extensive commentary on what AI is and is not. My approach here is pragmatic rather than theoretical.


There are two broad ways to define artificial intelligence. One is to describe how it works. The other is to describe what it can do. Technologists tend to take the first path. They characterize AI by referring to underpinning techniques and technologies. They often use specialist jargon. They will speak, for example, about ‘neural networks’ or ‘large language models’ or ‘backpropagation’ or ‘graphics processing units’ or ‘adversarial machine learning’ or ‘foundation models’. In doing so, they often bamboozle those who are listening. Most people—from decision makers to citizens—have little clue what these terms mean and don’t have the technical background to digest the underlying ideas easily.


Which is why I prefer to take the second path as my starting point. The best way to secure an early foothold in grasping what AI is all about is to be given some practical examples of what AI systems are already doing and are likely to be doing before long.


# What AI Can Do
In the early days of AI, in the era of what is sometimes called ‘good old-fashioned AI’ (‘GOFAI’), we chunked up artificial intelligence into a series of subfields. This was in the 1960s, 1970s, and 1980s. We used quite general labels and our focus was on broad categories of task for which AI was being used. One of these was speech recognition, which aimed to develop systems that could understand words spoken by humans—systems that could distinguish, for example, between ‘abominable’ and ‘a bomb in a bull’. Another was translating from one language to another, avoiding howlers like the conversion of the English phrase ‘out of sight, out of mind’ into Russian as ‘invisible insanity’. A related focus was ‘natural language processing’—enabling humans to instruct and communicate with computers in everyday human language, with all its ambiguities and imprecisions, rather than by code.


Another branch of AI was expert systems. The most popular approach here was to try to capture and model the apparent reasoning and problem-solving processes of the brightest humans and to embed these in systems. This would enable non-experts to work in various fields at the level of leading experts.


Work in four other subfields also proved to be foundational: one was game-playing, which continues to be a fertile testing ground for AI and the focus of some of the highest-profile advances, not least in chess and Go; another was robotics, which remains very challenging, although great progress has recently been made; a third was research into vision and perception, systems that could recognize objects (including faces) in the physical world. Lastly, there was automatic programming, systems that themselves could generate other systems. This last one may yet turn out to be the most significant of all, because it marks the shift from machines developed exclusively by humans to machines that can write their own code and so self-improve and even self-replicate.


The foundational era of GOFAI is behind us, and when we talk today about what AI can do, we tend to focus now on more particular tasks, activities, and fields. My own main interest is in the use of AI for professional work. In healthcare, for example, there are AI systems that can diagnose illnesses, interpret scans, recommend treatments, undertake robotic surgery, counsel patients, and assist in drug and vaccine development (AI accelerated the development of Moderna’s COVID mRNA vaccine).1 In law, there are systems that can draft, compare, summarize, and simplify legal documents, as well as offer guidance, answer questions, generate legal arguments, and predict the outcome of court decisions.2 In tax, systems can produce and file tax returns, and create plans and strategies that legitimately minimize tax liability. In education, AI is enabling personalized learning, providing ready access around the world to the finest teaching resources.3 In architecture, remarkable buildings are being designed by AI systems (and even being constructed using 3D printing).


Similar advances are being made in management consulting, audit, surveying, actuarial work, and in most other professional and related services, such as insurance, banking, and investment management. It is early days for many of these systems. Some are not entirely reliable, although they are improving by the day. Others can already match the performance of individual human experts. Yet others can outperform teams of even the brightest specialists.4


The use of AI extends well beyond professional and white-collar work—from participating in quizzes to managing power grids, from self-driving cars to co-piloting military aircraft, from optimizing business processes to scheduling plane routes to cut emissions, from determining humans’ emotional states to creating lifelike talking faces. Where humans with brains were once needed, AI is increasingly engaged.


While many AI systems are stand-alone and single-purpose (such as medical diagnostic systems), often and increasingly AI is embedded in other systems. For example, mobile phone manufacturers Apple and Samsung claim that their devices are now easier and better to use because they are enhanced by AI. At the same time, AI is being bundled into and integrated with everyday software. Microsoft provides its AI system, Copilot, along with its general Office software. This system can draft emails and documents, summarize meetings and email threads, generate presentation slides, create to-do lists, and much else.


In practice, this means that most of us will soon have at our fingertips our very own personal assistants and research assistants, helping us to organize our lives and keeping us fully informed. Likewise, we will have our own personal tutors, ready to train us on all sorts of subjects. More, we will have ever-improving AI systems that can answer questions on any topic, and systems that can generate text (polished letters, poetry, articles, lyrics), as well as music, cartoons, line drawings, landscapes, videos, and more. In this spirit, AI will empower humanity. It’s not just about making today’s white-collar workers more productive and efficient.


Much AI is already in daily use but hidden from us. When online retailers and email adverts suggest movies, books, clothes, or food that we might like, the underpinning technology here—often said loosely to be ‘algorithms’—is some form of AI. Likewise, AI is often running in the background when we make job, loan, and insurance applications. In all the major organizations on which we rely—from manufacturing to retail, transport to hospitals, banks to governments—AI systems are in varying stages of operation.


These systems are also being used to create new worlds in virtual reality and even to connect our brains to computers (see Chapter 11). Most ambitiously, some AI enthusiasts believe that artificial intelligence will come to the rescue of humanity and help us meet our most serious challenges—from climate change to cancer, poverty to conflict, space exploration to global education, crowded cities to the erosion of democracy.5


I hope I’ve said enough to allow us now to come up with some kind of tentative summary of what AI is all about. Bear in mind—and this says a lot about the field—AI scientists themselves disagree endlessly over definitions of artificial intelligence—when 400 AI experts were asked to select from a range of proposed definitions, the most popular was only accepted by 56 per cent of them.6


So, I start by saying that AI is a branch of digital technology that is devoted to the development and use of systems that can undertake tasks and engage in activities of a sort that we have traditionally thought could only be taken on by humans with our remarkable brains. We must go further now and add that AI systems are increasingly outperforming humans and doing things which are beyond the capabilities of humans. (There are some commentators and experts who want to go further still and say that a system is only proper AI if it can be said to be ‘conscious’. However, for reasons I lay out later, I think this is not necessary and can cause confusion.)


One impact of AI systems as just described is that they raise new questions in traditional fields. In education, for example, we are wondering what we are training young people to become. In the investment community, venture capital and private equity specialists are questioning as never before whether businesses in which they are investing belong to industries that might be entirely upended by the time they will want to cash in. In the military, the possibility of autonomous weapons is requiring strategists to revisit basic doctrines of deterrence and defence.7 The AI infrastructure of nation states is coming to be regarded as a potential source of military advantage as well as economic differentiation.


The advance of AI should also be contemplated in all other fields that take a long view. This doesn’t always happen. In discussions about climate change, for example, while a common focus is 2050, rarely have I heard discussion about the relevance of AI for this conversation. There is concern, of course, about AI’s increasing hunger for power, but a bigger question perhaps is the extent to which AI systems will have changed civilization and humanity by 2050. According to this book at least, the human condition might be wildly different by that date and our systems may be unfathomably more capable than humans. This does not diminish the climate challenge, but it does encourage us to take a wider view of what we might want by 2050.


AI also, of course, gives rise to a wide range of forbidding risks, threats, and vulnerabilities. These are addressed in Chapters 8 and 9.


My focus so far, then, is on what AI can and might do. Our next port of call is the question of how AI works. I tackle this, in non-technical terms, by discussing how artificial intelligence has evolved.


# A Very Short History of AI
I should first explain why I choose to steer clear of the detailed operations of AI systems. I do so partly because this book is aimed at a general and so non-technical readership, but, more importantly, because the enabling techniques and technologies are changing rapidly. In my view, insight into the bowels of the latest technologies will in the long run be less valuable for most people than insight into what these systems are likely actually to do in the years ahead. New waves of systems and methods will come into vogue. Better than trying to grasp the intricacies of today’s techniques is to have a broader sense of the way in which AI has evolved over the years; better to have a grip on the history and trajectory of AI. To secure this understanding, to give a feel for the advances made in AI, my next step is to run through various landmark achievements in AI over the past seventy-five years. This is largely a personal view: the story of AI through those advances that I regard as the highlights.


Most people know something about AI from science fiction. Some of the finest authors—such as Isaac Asimov, Neal Stephenson, William Gibson, Philip K. Dick, Arthur C. Clarke—anticipated many of the current and emerging uses of artificial intelligence.8 They foresaw the dangers too. And alongside various TV shows and movies—especially 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968), Blade Runner (1982), The Terminator (1984), and The Matrix (1999)—these writers have to a large extent shaped our expectations and fears of machines and robots to come.


AI is not confined to contemporary movies, TV, and fiction. There were hints of artificial intelligence in Classical antiquity—in Homer’s Iliad, in the workshop of Hephaestus, who made weapons for the gods, there were ‘handmaidens wrought of gold in the semblance of living maids’.9 More than two and a half thousand years later, there were some notable forays into AI in the nineteenth century, most famously in Mary Shelley’s Frankenstein (1818) and in Erewhon (1872) by Samuel Butler. There was also related fiction in the early twentieth century—for example, in the works of H. G. Wells, most memorably in the War of the Worlds (1895–7). Some decades later, in 1984 (published in 1949), George Orwell set the scene for much of the current conversation about widespread electronic surveillance of citizens in society.


AI became non-fiction in 1950, with the publication of Alan Turing’s seminal paper ‘Computing Machinery and Intelligence’, where he methodically explored the question ‘Can machines think?’10 (I return to this paper in Chapter 10.)


A few years later, in 1956, a group of scientists assembled in Dartmouth College, New Hampshire, in the US, for a summer workshop. The term ‘artificial intelligence’ was coined in a funding request written for that event. The main author of the paper was John McCarthy, now widely acknowledged as the person who came up with the term. The Dartmouth event was a gathering of talented Americans.11 The first international conference on AI was a symposium on the ‘mechanization of thought processes’ held in late 1958 at the National Physical Laboratory in Teddington, England.12


The earliest operational AI system was largely developed before the term ‘artificial intelligence’ was coined. This was the ‘Logic Theorist’, conceived in 1955 and completed in 1956 by Alan Newell, a computer scientist, and Herbert Simon, an economist. The innovation here was their development of a system that went beyond the number crunching, arithmetic, and codebreaking for which computers had been used until that time. Instead, this system engaged in a form of non-numeric, symbolic reasoning which to some extent simulated human problem-solving. Their system discovered proofs for theorems in symbolic logic.13 It was this system that Simon was describing—‘boastfully’, by his own admittance—in the quotation that appears at the start of this book.14


Another seed was also planted in this foundational era. John von Neumann, in a series of written lectures, which he didn’t live to complete and deliver, undertook the first sustained mathematical analysis of the relationships between the biological brain and the computer.15 Not perhaps a household name, von Neumann was nonetheless a Goliath of twentieth-century mathematics and physics, with an inestimable impact on the development of computers and AI. His thinking permeates this and most other books on the subject. Computers of today are still built according to what is known as the ‘von Neumann architecture’. In the 1950s, at the Institute for Advanced Study at Princeton, von Neumann worked in the same corridors as Albert Einstein, Kurt Gödel, and Robert Oppenheimer. In this company, it was von Neumann himself who ‘was called “The Great Man”’.16 One of my favourite von Neumann stories—possibly apocryphal, but a good laugh in any event—is that when he was 4 or 5, he asked his mother, who was smoking a cigarette and gazing out of a window, ‘Mommy, what are you calculating?’17


The 1960s brought various AI breakthroughs, but two stand out for me. The first was the DENDRAL initiative, led at Stanford University by Edward Feigenbaum. He captured and represented in various systems the expertise of Joshua Lederberg (Nobel laureate in medicine) and Carl Djerassi (the inventor of the birth control pill), and the resultant system was able, at a level higher than any human, to determine the structure of unknown molecules when presented with mass spectroscopic data. DENDRAL successfully implemented emerging techniques of machine learning (see below) and heuristics (rules of thumb which combined with formal knowledge to ensure expert-level performance).


My second milestone of the 1960s was the development of a system called ELIZA by Joseph Weizenbaum, then an AI professor at MIT (Massachusetts Institute of Technology). Named after the character Eliza Doolittle in George Bernard Shaw’s Pygmalion, the program was written to some extent tongue in cheek. It mimicked an interaction between a patient and a human psychotherapist. When Weizenbaum invited his secretary to use the system, he was deeply troubled when she asked him to leave the room so she could use it in private. Indeed he felt compelled partly by this incident to write what is still one of the finest books on the relationship between human beings and machines—Computer Power and Human Reason (1984).18 Anticipating much of the current ethical debate about AI, he warned there of the dangers of increasingly capable machines and their effects on humanity.


Inspired to a large extent by DENDRAL, the 1970s was a decade of great optimism among AI scientists. They busied themselves in converting theory into a flurry of practical applications, which were early ‘expert systems’. One system, PROSPECTOR, helped geologists to locate ore deposits, and it was claimed that it led directly to a molybdenum find valued at $100 million. The best-known systems of that era performed medical diagnoses and recommended therapy—MYCIN for certain infectious diseases, CADUCEUS in the field of internal medicine, and CASNET for glaucoma. These systems were all said to perform at expert-level or higher.19


Despite the early optimism and advances, AI endured troughs as well as peaks in the 1970s. The term ‘AI winter’ is often used to refer to a period when there was a significant dip in interest and investment in artificial intelligence. Sceptics and Luddites always rejoice in an AI winter. The first of these occurred in the late 1970s. This cooling-off followed cuts in public spending, especially in the US, and reflected a view that the available technology, although fascinating, was unlikely to deliver practical results and fulfil the early promise claimed by the pioneers. AI had not convinced the market.


Winter turned to spring, however, with the advent in the 1980s of ‘rule-based expert systems’ technology, which came at that time to be regarded as the applied and commercial branch of AI. I regard expert systems as the first generation of practical AI systems. Quite quickly, the business community began to see how they might be used in earnest across many sectors, including law, finance, banking, insurance, retail, manufacturing, and more.


The core idea with these systems was to create decision trees or flow charts that were thought, in very broad terms, to copy the lines of reasoning that human specialists followed in their work. Inexpert users were able to navigate these decision trees. The systems asked users questions, and provided answers and generated documentation, again at expert level.


I lived and breathed these systems in the 1980s. After writing my doctorate on the subject in 1986, I co-developed (from 1987 to 1988) the world’s first fully operational, commercial AI system for lawyers with Phillip Capper, a leading legal expert. Our system advised when a legal action could no longer be raised in the courts because too much time had passed. In developing the system, Phillip and I created a huge decision tree, with more than two million paths, representing all permutations of fact patterns and legal problems that we could imagine in this difficult branch of law. Broadly, we reduced his interpretation of the relevant legislation and case law to a complex computer program—an explicit representation of his knowledge in the form of code. Our system reduced research time from hours to minutes and offered explanations of its lines of reasoning. More than a third of a century on, Phillip still happily concedes that the system outperformed him.20


Then, in the late 1980s and early 1990s, came the second AI winter. This is often attributed to the difficulty and cost of industrializing expert systems, and sometimes to the collapse in the market for dedicated AI hardware. I have a different perspective. I believe the birth of the World Wide Web precipitated that winter. Certainly in the professions in the early 1990s, AI experts saw the ease with which content and services could be made widely available on an online basis. In turn, most of us working on AI in support of professional work transitioned for some years to developing tools and applications for the web. We accepted they were not as sophisticated as the complex rule-based expert systems we had been labouring over, but there was an immediacy, a commerciality, and a pragmatism to web development that seduced much of the AI community.


Many of us gave little thought to AI during the early to mid-1990s. But in 1997, when we learned that the world chess champion, Garry Kasparov, had been beaten by an IBM computer system, Deep Blue, we were amazed. Back in the 1980s, we thought this could never happen. Our only way then of delivering practical AI was to assemble huge decision trees that captured the apparent lines of reasoning of human experts, as we had done in law. However, leading experts—chess players and doctors, for example—argued that, in the most testing circumstances that confronted them, their expertise and experience couldn’t be reduced to mere flow charts. Their best moves or diagnoses appeared to depend, they felt, on some kind of ineffable, tacit knowledge—intuition or gut reaction—that couldn’t be codified in any computer system. This led us in the 1980s to believe there was and always would be a firm division of labour between machines and humans—for routine work, machines would be best suited, but when imagination and creativity were required, this would always be the unique province of humans.


Many people still believe this today. But it’s wrong. It was a mistake back in 1997, because we didn’t grasp the impact of the exponential increase in the processing power of computers (see Chapter 2). When Deep Blue beat Kasparov, that system was able to explore more than 300 million possible moves in one second. The finest chess players can contemplate about 110 moves at any given moment. Kasparov wasn’t overcome by a system that played as he did. He was thwarted in large part by brute-force processing.21


But we were wrong for another reason to think that machines would be confined for all time to undertaking routine activities. It turned out that a different way was found to develop systems that could achieve human expert-level performance. This led to what I call second-generation AI systems.


The first generation, the expert systems, required human ‘knowledge engineers’ to mine the jewels from the heads of ‘domain experts’ and convert their knowledge into decision trees. In truth, the clever thing about these systems was the human endeavour of building these flow charts. The operational systems themselves were like navigation aids. In contrast, second-generation AI systems themselves do much more of the heavy lifting. Rather than eliciting knowledge directly from humans, the second generation involves mining jewels from vast oceans of data. It shouldn’t surprise us that all the digital data we have amassed—on the web, in our systems, and across our databases, both in textual and multimedia format—captures, embodies, reflects, and contains so much of the combined and accumulated experience, ideas, insights, expertise, and emotions of humanity. The technological leap was to develop systems that can directly detect patterns, trends, and relationships in these oceans of data. The aim has been to develop computer systems that are able, in a variety of ways, to ‘learn’ from massive bodies of data without needing to be explicitly programmed—hence the phrase ‘machine learning’. These systems are often said to be ‘trained’ to perform certain types of task, from diagnosing illnesses to drafting complex documents. The main enabling technique in support of machine learning is known as ‘neural networks’ or ‘neural nets’. Much of the pioneering work here was done by Nobel laureate Geoffrey Hinton in the 1980s, although his paper on ‘deep learning’ in 2006 is often seen as a particular watershed in AI.22 Neural nets, as the name suggests, are inspired by the structure of the human brain. Current systems do not replicate brains. They are ‘idealizations’ of how brains seem to work.23


An early high-profile success with a form of machine learning came in 2011, when IBM had another breakthrough. On a live broadcast of Jeopardy!, a US TV quiz show, their Watson system (named after the founder of IBM, Thomas J. Watson), trounced the two best-ever human contestants. Effectively, this AI system was able to answer any question under the sun more speedily and accurately than any human. Watson was a mix of first-generation AI, in which the knowledge was explicitly coded, and second-generation, which relied on machines learning from large quantities of data.24 AI enthusiasts began to feel the warmth of a new summer.


Then, a few years later, came AlphaGo which remains one of my favourite examples of a high-performing machine learning system (although there is some first-generation AI in there too). It was designed originally by DeepMind (then a subsidiary of Google) to play the complex game Go—so complex that it has more possible moves than there are atoms in the universe. AI experts had previously doubted that any AI system could play Go to a high standard. But in early 2016, AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol, a world-class Go player, by four games to one. The system used ‘deep neural networks’ and was trained through both ‘supervised learning’ (based on past games of human experts) and ‘reinforcement learning’ (it self-improved by playing itself millions of times).25


AI enthusiasts talk a lot about the thirty-seventh move in the second game of that 2016 AlphaGo match. It was AlphaGo’s move, and no human being had thought of it before. A leading human player described it as ‘beautiful’ and said it brought a tear to his eye. Had the move been played by a human, we would have called it ‘creative’ or ‘innovative’ or even ‘genius’. But it was none of these things. It came about through a combination of massive processing power and remarkable algorithms that operated on vast bodies of data. Notably, some of the moves made by AlphaGo were well beyond the contemplation of those who designed the system.


Some commentators observed that although AlphaGo was a remarkable system, it relied mainly on the patterns of past expert human games and wouldn’t have performed as it did without building on the original human talent. Then came AlphaGo Zero in 2017. It was not fed any past human games. Rather, it was taught the rules of the game and left to work out for itself the optimum strategies and tactics. AlphaGo Zero beat the original AlphaGo by 100 games to nil.26 A version of AlphaGo Zero called AlphaZero went on to defeat world champion programs in chess, shogi, and Go.


In 2018, from the same stable then came AlphaFold. According to Nobel prize-winning scientist Venki Ramakrishnan, this was ‘a stunning advance on the protein-folding problem, a 50-year-old grand challenge in biology. It has occurred decades before many people in the field would have predicted.’27 This achievement was recognized in 2024—two AI scientists who led the development of AlphaFold, Demis Hassabis and John Jumper, were jointly awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry ‘for protein structure prediction’. AlphaFold and its successors should have a profound impact on curing disease and handling industrial waste.


# Later Breakthroughs
While DeepMind were working on the first version of AlphaFold, a group of AI scientists elsewhere in Google published a paper that brought about yet another step change in the advance of AI.28 This gave birth to a new ‘deep learning’ architecture for neural nets, known as ‘transformers’. This is the architecture that ‘large language models’ are built upon. These systems are ‘trained’ on huge volumes of data. Again, we won’t worry too much about the specific terminology and how they work. But let’s digest the words of Stephen Wolfram, who noted of this new architecture that ‘to everyone’s surprise—a neural net…is capable of doing what was thought to be that uniquely human thing of generating meaningful human language’.29


Using the transformer architecture, on 30 November 2022, OpenAI, then an American research laboratory, launched ChatGPT, the best-known large language model. While I had been following and had written about earlier versions of the GPT family, I was stunned by this new system (GPT stands for Generative Pre-trained Transformer). It was the most remarkable breakthrough I’d seen in my forty years of working on AI. The launch of ChatGPT was also a milestone in public recognition of the potential of AI systems. Suddenly, it seemed, we had a system that could provide concrete replies to almost any question asked in ordinary language. More amazingly, when it was invited to draft some text, a response would appear within seconds—perhaps a sonnet, a note of condolence, or maybe an extended essay on any given topic. The range and output seemed without limit.


Like many similar systems that have since been made available (for example, Gemini from Google, Bing from Microsoft, Claude from Anthropic), ChatGPT is a chatbot, which means it mimics human conversation. It’s also classified as a ‘generative’ AI system, which means it produces content on demand. And not just text—these systems can generate remarkable art, music, and video. They can even produce high-quality code—they can write software.


One vital point about ChatGPT is often ignored. The first version was an experimental initial stab. ChatGPT and generative AI were significant not for what they were in late 2022 or for what they are today (mightily impressive but sometimes defective) but as harbingers of what later generations of these systems are likely to become. It’s still early days. Crucially, though, the pace of change and adoption is accelerating and, as discussed in Chapter 2, we can reasonably expect these systems to be increasingly more capable and reliable.


ChatGPT and generative AI are the latest chapter of an ongoing story that stretches back to the pioneering work of the 1950s. The latest systems do not replace older AI systems, such as expert systems and earlier predictive systems. Nor, crucially, are they the endgame. The field of artificial intelligence is and will continue to be made up of a cumulative series of techniques, the most recent of which is generative AI.


AI has been around now for seventy-five years or so. In the first few decades, breakthroughs came every five or ten years. Now, they seem to be emerging every six to twelve months, some as technological innovations, others as dramatic new applications. As I write, in 2024, it’s hard to keep pace with the changes—both in the underpinning technologies and in the range of use. Today, we are enjoying a blistering AI summer. Some argue that the current hype and scale of investment amount to an artificial and temporary overheating, but no one can sensibly deny the extraordinary level of interest and investment. My view is that we will be basking in an AI summer for the foreseeable future.


No areas of human life seem to be unaffected. Where will it all end? How much more advanced could AI systems become? Are we close to creating some kind of ‘superintelligence’? Are we approaching some kind of ‘singularity’? What do these terms actually mean? I explain them in Chapter 2 after placing AI in the broader context of digital technology.
