# Chapter 2
# On Technology
To grasp the long-term significance of AI, generative or otherwise, it helps to zoom out and gain a sense of what is happening more generally in the wider world of technology, by which I mean digital technology. Useful too, I believe, is to position advances in technology and AI in the wider context of various stages of human and societal development. It also helps to unearth a couple of biases that can distort our grasp of technological advance. Thus equipped, we can then begin to answer the question—how advanced might AI systems eventually become?


# Digital Technology
Technologists have many ways of explaining the extraordinary era in which we live and labour. I do so under five headings.


The first and most important concept to grasp is that our machines are becoming increasingly capable.1 Forget for a moment about machine learning and quantum computing and all the other enabling technologies and concepts that are bandied around. Focus instead on the fact that our systems and machines, quite simply, are doing more and more. This observation is of the essence. Almost daily, it seems, we hear of remarkable new breakthroughs, systems, tools, technologies, apps, and start-ups. Much of this news is about systems that are undertaking tasks that, until recently, we thought were the exclusive province of human beings—from medical diagnostic tools to systems that can produce paintings, from systems that can tell whether a human smile is fake to those that can predict how many years you have left to live.


We have for long accepted that diggers, chainsaws, and cranes can outperform us in undertaking the types of tasks that we take on with our hands in our gardens and on building sites. We’ve come to terms with cars travelling faster than we can run and, for generations now, we know that combine harvesters can gather crops more rapidly than human labourers. In our factories, we’ve known for decades that machines are much more productive and safer than humans for many activities.


We seem much less ready to concede that machines might rival or outclass humans when it comes to activities and tasks that call upon our intellect, including our abilities to think, reason, solve problems, hypothesize, and plan. However, in the digital era, the overall trajectory is clear—it is likely that many or most of the activities and tasks that we’ve felt could only be performed by humans will, as time rolls on, be taken on autonomously by capable systems, or by less expert humans with the support of these systems. Some examples are laid out in Chapter 1. The achievements of current and emerging systems suggest not only that our systems are becoming increasingly capable but that they are advancing at an apparently accelerating rate. In many fields, they are already outperforming human beings.


My second fundamental observation about technology is that the remarkable array of enabling techniques and technologies that are fuelling our machines are advancing exponentially. Whether we’re talking about processing power, memory, bandwidth, data storage capacity, or a whole host of other enablers, the rate of change is explosive and often hard to fathom. Take neural networks, the technology that underlies most current AI systems. The computational power available to ‘train’ these systems doubled every six months or so between 2010 and 2022.2 The advance in chip technology has been spectacular.3 This means that, every ten years or so, at this rate, the computation speeds will improve about a millionfold. While we can and should be amazed by recent developments such as ChatGPT and other generative AI systems, we should perhaps be more stunned by the fact that the technology that underpins these systems might itself advance a millionfold in the coming decade.


We are living in an era of greater technological change than humanity has ever witnessed. More than this, as noted, the pace of change itself is accelerating. Ray Kurzweil, a leading inventor and futurist, formalizes our understanding of this speeding-up in his ‘law of accelerating returns’. This is the idea that ‘Information technologies like computing get exponentially cheaper because each advance makes it easier to design the next stage of their own evolution.’4 Emerging systems themselves play a role in designing and developing the next developments, and so on. The clearest recent illustration of this is found in those generative AI systems that can write code: systems that develop systems. This is of profound significance for the future of technology and humanity—when systems can self-correct, self-improve, and self-extend, we should expect even more rapid advance than we’ve seen so far. The impact will be both on current systems and in the propagation of entirely new systems, for example, in creating massive, evolving online virtual worlds.


In all, in the advance of enabling technologies, we are beyond the ‘knee’ of the exponential curve and should expect explosive further advances across the entire realm of digital technology.


Thirdly, there is no apparent finishing line in the global competition to develop digital technologies. No one in the world’s leading tech companies or in research laboratories in the US, China, or South Korea is expecting the tech job to be over soon. There’s no final destination in sight. Rather, it’s a relentless race without end.


AI has captured the market’s imagination and captivated the investor community. Remarkable levels of capital are being injected. The major tech and AI companies themselves are expending eye-watering sums on research, governments are disbursing massively, most large businesses are allocating significant funds to piloting and development, while the vibrant and talented AI start-up community is benefiting from great infusions of venture capital. Private equity houses too are joining the fray, on the hunt for the biggest of plays. A study by the accounting firm PwC set AI’s potential contribution to the global economy by 2030 at $15.7 trillion, and that was in 2017, before the coming of generative AI.5 In 2023, the management consultants McKinsey found that generative AI alone stands ‘to add up to £4.4 trillion to the global economy—annually’.6 We can quibble about the number of trillions, but market sentiment is clear—AI is a phenomenon of enormous potential economic value. In turn, many of the best graduates and brightest scientists have been unable to resist the gravitational pull of the research funding, the high-end salaries, and the investment capital that are accompanying AI businesses and projects.


The rapid emergence of new systems is evidence of the scale of activity. Consider ChatGPT, the flagship for the latest wave of AI systems. Within four months of its launch a major upgrade emerged (v.4), as did a whole slew of credible competitors. By the time you read this book, I expect GPT-5 will be available.


New techniques are also flourishing. One example is work on ‘synthetic data’. This sounds rather circular, but the idea is that machines can generate great bodies of data that can then power future AI systems. This would reduce the reliance on the legally questionable process of scraping data from the web and from existing databases.7 Another example is the development of systems that no longer require their users to be ‘prompt engineers’. Instead, the systems themselves are able to ask users questions systematically—to help pin down, categorize, and classify the problems or issues on which guidance is sought. Or there is the possibility of autonomous AI systems that are able to communicate with one another, to take decisions, or to access and deploy computer or physical resources without direct human involvement (sometimes called ‘agentic AI’).


All of this brings me, fourthly, to a notion that I find at once exhilarating and yet deeply unsettling. Assuming that humanity is not wiped out in some planet-wide catastrophe (self-induced or through external factors), the launch of the most powerful and impactful digital technologies most likely lies in the years ahead. I call these the not yet invented technologies. Digital technology has only been with us for eighty-five years or so. We’re still warming up. In not many years, our current technologies will look primitive, much as our 1980s kit appears antiquated today.


In the future, human lives will be transformed by technologies that have not yet been invented or even imagined. The most impactful systems await our discovery and development. I can’t prove this, of course, but our experience of the last few decades (from the emergence of the web to social media to generative AI) strongly supports the claim.


Under this heading, we should allow, at least in concept, for both conceivable and inconceivable AI systems. I once heard the writer and thinker Edward de Bono informally explain a similar distinction. He said—and I paraphrase from memory—that for as long as humans had been able to lift their eyes to the skies, they could at least imagine walking on the moon. This was conceivable. What was inconceivable until the second quarter of the twentieth century was that on the day humans did actually walk on the moon, this would be watched by more than 650 million people on little boxes in the corner of their sitting rooms. So too with AI—there is much that we can currently imagine and conceive but there will be much more that is simply beyond our current contemplation.


Many newcomers to the field of AI seem to believe that the recent inventions—ChatGPT and the rest—represent the endgame, the peak of technological advance. Instead, they are our faltering first infant steps. Most predictions about the future are in my view irredeemably flawed because they ignore the not yet invented technologies.


Fifthly, finally, and most disconcertingly of all in my summary of the era of digital technology—we already don’t fully understand how our most advanced AI systems work. And it is likely that this state of incomprehension will grow. This hit home for me when I read What Is ChatGPT Doing…and Why Does It Work?,8 by Stephen Wolfram, one of the leading computer scientists of our time. I was struck by how often he concedes that he cannot explain how ChatGPT works (and this was GPT-3.5—newer versions have since been launched). Time and again, Wolfram describes ChatGPT’s performance as ‘remarkable’ and its output as ‘unexpected’.9 He goes further. Our understanding of how these systems work, he argues, is still pre-theoretical—we do not have general, systematic, scientific ways of explaining how ChatGPT and similar systems do what they do. Wolfram even writes of circumstances when ‘a bit of voodoo begins to creep in’.10 He speaks of the ‘art’ of training neural networks,11 and of one key technique he says, ‘I don’t think there’s any particular science to this. It’s just that various different things have been tried, and this is one that seems to work’ and ‘without really needing to “understand at an engineering level” quite how the neural net has ended up configuring itself’.12 In summary, Wolfram says we are ‘discovering, yes, it’s complicated in there, and we don’t understand it—even though in the end it’s producing recognizable human language’.13


This should humble all who think they know what’s going on when they use ChatGPT and similar systems. When systems attain a certain level of complexity, their individual daily operations (as distinct from their general design) often defy human explanation and understanding. We saw this at the turn of the millennium with the Y2K problem. Non-technical observers reasonably asked why computer specialists couldn’t tell us what would happen when the clock struck midnight. Our systems, even then, seemed too complex to fathom. AI systems take this incomprehension to a new level.


To recap on the digital era—our systems and machines are becoming increasingly capable; the underpinning technologies are advancing at an explosive, exponential rate; there is no apparent finishing line; we haven’t invented most of the technologies that will change our future lives; and even today we don’t fully understand the operations of some of our AI systems.


# Four, Five, or Even Six Stages of Human Progress
Another way of looking at the impact of digital technology is by reference to the main way that information is captured, shared, and communicated in society.14 I follow anthropologists like Walter Ong who observe humans progressing through various stages: the age of orality, when communication was dominated by spoken words; the era of script; then of print; and today a world in which communication is very largely enabled by a variety of digital technologies.15 Depending on how things unfold, it is likely there will be a fifth—perhaps the epoch of transhumanism (see Chapters 11 and 12)—when humanity, technology, robotics, molecular nanotechnology, virtual reality, synthetic biology, and genetic engineering converge. And there could, at least conceivably, be a sixth stage, when AI systems might replace us entirely (see Chapter 12).


In my estimation, we are presently edging our way to the end of a transitional phase between the third and fourth stages of development, between a print-based industrial society and an AI-enabled digital society. This is not a minor shift. Some liken its impact to the scale of change brought by the advent of the printing press, which was the technology that enabled the shift from the second to the third stage of human progress. I can see the analogy in that movable-type machines led to a revolution in education, in the spread of ideas, and in the global market for reading. As we move into a fully fledged AI-based digital society, underpinned by the internet and the web, and delivered through ubiquitous mobile devices (and soon through brain/computer interfaces—see Chapter 11), there will indeed be a further colossal change in the way we create, store, disseminate, and consume information. But the stuff of AI is much more than data and information. AI systems don’t simply store and distribute knowledge—they apply that knowledge.


There has been another shift in the transition from the print-based to the digital society. Over the past eighty-five years or so, computer scientists and systems designers have put layer upon layer between humans and the 1s and 0s of digital machines. In the beginning, computers could only be operated directly by engineers writing in low-level programming languages. Over time, higher-level programming languages were developed which were easier to use and execute. For users, developments such as graphical user interfaces and voice recognition have enabled people with no technological knowledge to use computers, including tablets and handhelds. Much of the computer industry has been devoted to making the technology accessible and usable for ordinary mortals as well as the computer science priesthood. With advances in AI and the ability to communicate with our machines in natural language, the gap between humans and 1s and 0s has now almost been closed. It will shut completely with the advent of virtual reality and brain/computer interfaces. This will represent a fundamental and irreversible inflection point in the story of human civilization.


# Some Biases
In thinking about digital technology and AI, there are two common but deeply unhelpful biases which often obscure our view of what is possible or likely in the future.


I call the first one ‘technological myopia’, which is a tendency, when evaluating the long-term potential of a new technique or technology, to pay excessive attention to the current version and its current limitations. Some critics of generative AI, for example, will point to the fact that today’s systems ‘hallucinate’. This happens when these systems provide apparently credible information or insights that are wrong or misleading. This is certainly a constraint in 2024, and it is important that the limitations of today’s systems are widely understood. Those who dispense appropriate warnings provide useful insight.


But we must view with great caution the pathological doubters and the po-faced social scientists who relentlessly rehearse the shortcomings and flaws of yesterday’s systems. They are seemingly oblivious to the exponential trajectories, to the relentless doublings. It is wrong-headed and often irresponsible to give the sense that current defects will be problems for all time, as I am afraid some AI sceptics often do. They could be, but it is much more likely that the industry will work hard and overcome ‘hallucinations’ and other current problems. As a generality, today’s versions are the worst they are ever going to be.


The idea that current systems will remain as they are, warts and all, for the foreseeable future is the least likely future when the technologies in question are attracting great interest in the market. Long-term strategic planning and policymaking should, of course, be grounded, but it will be worthless in AI if it is too focused on today’s systems and their limitations.


The second bias is what I call ‘irrational rejectionism’, which rears its unworthy head whenever critics dogmatically dismiss the relevance, utility, or potential of particular systems without taking the trouble to see them in action. Too often, for instance, I hear people speaking unfavourably about ChatGPT, and I am amazed to learn, after a little probing—OK, cross-examination—that they haven’t actually used the system themselves. Irrational rejectionism is a disorder most commonly found in busy leaders and busy experts beyond the field of AI. It is intolerable. If you are too busy to view a system in operation, your evaluations are not simply lacking in merit; they are deeply disrespectful.


# Future Capability
To return now to the question raised in Chapter 1 about where it will all end, my general response as laid out earlier is that there is no apparent finishing line. There is no end state. It’s wrong to imagine there will be a final version of AI. Tucked away behind this question, however, is a deep unease that is surfacing in common conversation. How capable might our machines become? Might they even, in some sense, take over?

I’ve derived these hypotheses both from the AI literature and from countless conversations with specialists who are working on the commercial exploitation of AI. In Chapter 12, I add a sixth hypothesis, which I call the ‘AI Evolution Hypothesis’, but we have work to do before this revelation.


The ‘Hype Hypothesis’ holds that we are coming to the end of the AI road. It concedes there have been some remarkable advances but insists that there are also some inherent limitations of AI and that these amount to permanent roadblocks. This hypothesis contends that we will see some modest but not substantial improvements to our current AI, but these systems will never be entirely reliable, and their underlying data is irredeemably flawed and biased. Underpinning this view is a deep scepticism and mistrust of the noise and excitement that has attended recent developments.


Next, is the ‘GenAI+ Hypothesis’. This suggests that the current wave of generative AI systems will be significantly upgraded and improved, but we are unlikely to see any huge further breakthroughs in AI. We will, therefore, move beyond hype and experiment to reliable, widespread daily use, while most hallucinations and biases will be overcome. On this view, an improved version of today’s generative AI will be fully integrated into the operations of most organizations, securing notable efficiency and productivity gains. It will also empower many citizens, providing them with administrative, research, learning, and problem-solving resources. This hypothesis has taken the business community by storm.


Then there is the ‘AGI Hypothesis’. AGI refers here to ‘artificial general intelligence’. Consider the definition offered by Michael Wooldridge, a seasoned AI scholar, who says that ‘AGI roughly equates to having a computer that has the full range of intellectual capabilities that a person has—this would include the ability to converse in natural language…solve problems, reason, perceive its environment and so on, at or above the same level as a typical person.’16 This essentially means full-scale, human-level performance by machines. We might more strictly refer to this as a ‘Near-AGI Hypothesis’, because we can imagine AI systems that can match or exceed the level of humans at almost all cognitive tasks but fall short on a small number. AGI and near-AGI systems would be considerably more advanced than today’s generative AI systems, whose range is still limited. AGI represents a major step change up from the performance of current generative AI.


Another hypothesis is that our machines will at some stage achieve ‘superintelligence’, a term popularized in 2014 by Nick Bostrom.17 He defines superintelligence as ‘any intellect that greatly exceeds the cognitive performance of humans in virtually all domains of interest’.18 But how ‘greatly’ is ‘greatly’? For James Lovelock, who prefers the term ‘hyperintelligence’, our systems will ‘become thousands then millions of times more intelligent than us’.19 The ‘Superintelligence Hypothesis’ that I have in mind envisages that AI systems will in due course become unfathomably more capable than humans.


Finally, there is the ‘Singularity Hypothesis’, whose best-known proponent is Ray Kurzweil. In his latest account, he foresees that ‘we will merge with AI and augment ourselves with millions of times the computational power that our biology gave us. This will expand our intelligence and consciousness so profoundly that it’s difficult to comprehend.’20 He borrows the term ‘singularity’ from mathematics and physics but stresses that he uses it metaphorically, ‘because it captures our inability to comprehend such a radical shift in our current level of intelligence’.21 He goes on to project ‘a process of co-creation—evolving our minds to unlock deeper insight, and using those powers to produce transcendent new ideas for our future minds to explore’.22


Which should we believe—the Hype Hypothesis, the GenAI+ Hypothesis, the AGI Hypothesis, the Superintelligence Hypothesis, or the Singularity Hypothesis? The AI community itself—computer scientists, cognitive scientists, social scientists, and philosophers—is divided on this question. Some seem to expect a slowdown in technological advance; others anticipate that the pace of change will continue to accelerate indefinitely. Some regard the superintelligence and singularity hypotheses as little more than science fiction;23 others regard great technological leaps as all but inevitable. I wish I could reassure readers who are wanting to future-proof their lives or businesses or the careers of their children. But there is no agreed and settled future.


How, then, should we think about this? First of all, we should distinguish between the advance and the adoption of technology. Although the advance of the enabling techniques and technologies can currently be mapped onto an apparently smooth, explosive exponential curve, the pace of adoption and usage invariably follows a different growth path. Humans’ adoption of new technologies is better described in a more jagged, jumpy series of lines. In other words, even if some use of AI is technologically feasible, it may not be immediately or ever embraced because of, say, commercial, cultural, regulatory, political, or ethical factors. For now, humans still have the choice whether to accept and use any given AI system or not. This means the relative strength of each hypothesis cannot be judged on purely technological grounds.


Social scientists, market researchers, and policymakers urge us to be evidence-based in discussing possible futures of AI. But this will reveal only part of the unfolding story. Of course, we should gather and assess data about current systems. However, a snapshot of today’s technology and its limitations is incomplete if not also viewed in the broader context of the trajectory of advance in AI. Focusing on one point on the curve (for example, conducting research on one system at one moment in time) yields limited insight and often tempts commentators to technological myopia.


More importantly and obviously, there is no evidence from the future. This means we have to speculate as well as extrapolate. In that spirit, I offer some personal speculation here. I do so in light of many of the findings in this chapter—the trajectory of breakthroughs, the increasing capability of our systems, the exponential growth of underpinning technologies, the law of accelerating returns, the likely impact of not yet invented systems, the current and projected scale of investment, the evident market demand, the scale and energy of the AI start-up community, and the involvement of so many able and ambitious humans.


These factors lead me to find the Hype Hypothesis to be implausible. It projects a very unlikely future. In support of the Hype Hypothesis, those who speak of an impending AI winter or of the AI bubble bursting entirely, I believe are mistaken. Their error, I suspect, is not grasping current market appetite. Whatever exaggeration there might well be about AI leading imminently to pervasive disruption and irreversible transformation, we are already seeing AI secure significant efficiency and productivity gains. This is simply through the application of AI in automating the way businesses and governments currently operate (in Chapter 6 I explore the impact of AI beyond automation). It’s not fanciful or hyperbolic to claim that AI is already streamlining and optimizing everyday, routine, repetitive, administrative, process-based work and promises much more in this direction. There is great economic value here, as major consulting firms are avariciously explaining to all organizations with budgets. And the surface here has scarcely been scuffed.


In previous winters, in marked contrast, little new economic value was created through AI work. The investment was largely in unexploited research and development, and in piloting and experiment. But there was no compelling business case for carrying on. The market was enjoying no serious commercial return from AI. I was there and I saw it. Today, very differently, AI’s implementation is clearly destined to affect top and bottom lines. It is moving to the heart of the process improvement programmes of many businesses in many sectors. Its deployment is already being baked into the long-term plans for public services around the world. In my daily work as a strategy adviser, I see this clearly, even if it is not immediately visible in standard metrics. The market wants more, which will surely lead to greater innovation and advance. It may well be that AI will develop more slowly than many expect—the advance/adoption distinction again—but to imagine it will somehow plunge into the depths of winter is to ignore market forces.


Given the factors noted above, I also fully expect the GenAI+ Hypothesis to be corroborated during this decade. Here technological advance will run a couple of years ahead of adoption. And we should be very clear that GenAI+ that falls short of AGI can, nonetheless, deliver life-changing, life-enhancing, life-saving, and life-threatening AI.


What about the AGI Hypothesis? This is where it gets interesting. The AI community’s view on this has changed since the launch of ChatGPT. Up until late 2022, when asked about the possibility of developing AGI systems, most experts in artificial intelligence seemed to be saying that this would take from twenty to forty years, if ever. Ray Kurzweil was among a bolder group. Since 1999, he has been predicting that AGI would be with us by 2029.24


Since the emergence of generative AI systems, I’m finding the twenty to forty years has often shrunk to five to ten years. Strikingly, those who support the ‘scaling hypothesis’ believe that no new technological breakthroughs will be necessary to crack AGI—it will be sufficient to throw more ‘compute’ and data at the challenge.25 By ‘compute’, technologists mean computing resource, which broadly means processing power. (Technologists are happy to change verbs to nouns, incidentally.)


As for data, the fuel of today’s AI systems, the challenge here is to scoop up more and more—whether from existing sources (multimedia and not just text), or generated synthetically, or captured afresh. Other specialists claim that current AI techniques will not take us to AGI and that entirely new tools and methods are required. And some believe that AGI, for the foreseeable future, is the stuff of fiction.


In the grand arc of human history, whether AGI is achieved in five, ten, or fifty years is of little moment. My own view, considering the various trajectories, market appetite, and the ingenuity of the industry, is that we should plan for AGI (more precisely, near-AGI) arriving between 2030 and 2035. That is what I advise my private and public sector clients. I’m emphatically not claiming that we will definitely have AGI by then. But I think it a sufficiently plausible scenario that we should be thinking through its implications for humanity, society, and the market. Few people beyond the world of AI are thinking systematically and seriously about AGI. Most long-term planning of governments and businesses is premised unwittingly on the GenAI+ Hypothesis. This may prove to be short-sighted and, much worse, may expose us to enormous risks, which is why I call in Chapter 9 for much greater focus on the question ‘what-if-AGI?’


In everyday discussion about AI, I often find it assumed by thoughtful non-experts that the final AI destination—at some stage, usually in the distant future—will be machines that are just about as capable in many ways as bright humans. Somehow, it is presumed that, at a push, they will level off at around that level. Rarely is it seriously imagined that AI systems might become incalculably, unfathomably, ineffably more capable than even the best of the best humans. This takes us to the land of the Superintelligence Hypothesis.


On one view, if and when we have achieved AGI, then we are on a slippery slope that leads inexorably—and maybe very rapidly—to superintelligence. To wind back to the mid-1960s, Irving John Good set the hare racing on this argument in his paper ‘Speculations Concerning the First Ultraintelligent Machine’. Good was a mathematician who had worked on codebreaking with Alan Turing. To paraphrase and quote from a purple passage in that paper, once we have machines that can themselves design machines, these machines will design ‘ever better machines’ and ‘there would then unquestionably be an “intelligence explosion”…the intelligence of man would be left far behind…Thus the first ultraintelligent machine is the last invention that man need ever make’.26 The Superintelligence Hypothesis, following Good, does not posit humans as the creators of tomorrow’s most advanced machines. Rather, in crude terms, in an unsettling recursive vortex, systems create new and better systems, and these new systems themselves self-improve, and so on, until…well, we are not sure.


I prefer to think of what is envisaged here as a ‘capability explosion’ rather than an ‘intelligence explosion’, but, either way, the concept is unsettling. While I find a sudden leap to superintelligence to be hard to conceive, the logic of the move from AGI to superintelligence is more compelling. The big question is whether we are too late to turn back the tide.


I believe the genie is only half out of the bottle. The market is fickle, the focus for the next few years might be more on commercial exploitation than technological advance, and we are some years away from autonomous self-replicating, agentic systems that can set and change their own goals. But, vitally, we need to start thinking through the implications of AGI—now. This is why, in Chapter 9, I call for ‘What-if-AGI?’ thinking.


As for the Singularity Hypothesis, I hesitate to challenge Kurzweil because he has been remarkably prescient in the past about the progress of various digital technologies. For more than two decades, he has been predicting that the singularity will come to pass by 2045. Here, though, I lean on my distinction between advance and adoption. My main hesitation about the merger between humans and AI (which is central to the Singularity Hypothesis) is not about technological feasibility. My concern, rather, is over whether this is desirable and ethical. I return to this question in Chapter 12.


A word, finally, about terminology. As I explain in Chapter 5, I find the term ‘intelligence’, whether human or artificial, to be confusing. From here on—and I keep this intentionally open-textured—I use the phrase ‘massively capable systems’ to refer to systems that comfortably and sometimes spectacularly outperform human beings.
