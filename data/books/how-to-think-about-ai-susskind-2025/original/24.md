# Chapter 3
# Process-Thinking and Outcome-Thinking
In the aftermath of the launch of ChatGPT, I was struck by the published reactions of two remarkable public intellectuals—Henry Kissinger and Noam Chomsky. Both at the time were highly active American nonagenarians. Both were controversial iconoclasts. Both had armies of zealous supporters and fervent detractors.


# Two Cultures of AI
On the topic of AI, however, Kissinger and Chomsky seemed to be entirely at odds. Writing about ChatGPT in the Wall Street Journal in late February 2023, Kissinger effused with his eminent co-authors, declaring that generative AI ‘will redefine human knowledge, accelerate changes in the fabric of our reality, and reorganize politics and society…open revolutionary avenues for human reason and new horizons for consolidated knowledge’. For Kissinger and his collaborators, this latest wave of AI constituted an event as momentous for humanity as the invention of the printing press—‘a novel and spectacular achievement that stands as a glory to the human mind as AI’. In operation, ‘to the naive user, it seems like a supremely fast and highly articulate librarian-scholar coupled with a professorial savant. It facilitates the summary and interrogation of the world’s knowledge far more effectively than any existing technological or human interface.’1


Chomsky, on the other hand, also backed up by credible colleagues, assumed a very different posture. In the New York Times, he claimed in early March 2023 that ‘It is at once comic and tragic…that so much money and attention should be concentrated on so little a thing…Given the amorality, faux science and linguistic incompetence of these systems, we can only laugh or cry at their popularity.’ More, Chomsky and his collaborators feared that machine learning ‘will degrade our science and debase our ethics by incorporating into our technology a fundamentally flawed conception of language and knowledge’. They likened ChatGPT to ‘a lumbering statistical engine for pattern matching’. They dismissed the system as ‘something so trivial when contrasted with the human mind’ and advised that ‘predictions of machine learning systems will always be superficial and dubious’.2 A damning indictment.


Which is it—a change to the fabric of reality or so little a thing? How do we make sense of such radically different views? How can it be that two of the most fertile human minds have come to conclusions about machines that are so palpably at variance?


My answer is that Kissinger and Chomsky represent two very different perspectives and cultures in the world of AI. I call these process-thinking and outcome-thinking. This distinction has significant ramifications for the way we think about AI. I borrow the term ‘two cultures’ from C. P. Snow, who famously argued that ‘two polar groups’, the arts and sciences, ‘split the intellectual life of the whole of western society’.3 I see an analogous split in the world of AI, featuring process-thinkers and outcome-thinkers.


Process-thinkers are interested in how complex systems work. Outcome-thinkers are interested in the results they bring. Process-thinkers are interested in the architecture of systems. Outcome-thinkers concentrate on their function. Process-thinkers tend to be ‘bottom-up’ thinkers, intrigued by the operational details. Outcome-thinkers tend to be ‘top-down’ thinkers, preoccupied with overall impact. The mindsets of each are very different.


# Thinking Differently
The process-thinkers themselves fall largely into three categories. The first group is primarily interested in how AI systems work. They are preoccupied, for example, with algorithms, data, machine architecture, computer processing, neural networks, graphics processing units, machine learning, weights, tokens, and much else. They tend to be technologists, software developers, and data scientists. While these engineers might fall into different camps, favouring one technique or technology over others, they have in common a deep engagement with the actual design and operations of machines.


A second type of process-thinker in artificial intelligence is primarily interested in how the human mind and brain work. This insight can help us in the design and development of AI systems. It can also reveal limitations in the machines we are building. Included in this camp are neuroscientists, neuropsychologists, cognitive scientists, and philosophers.


Still other process-thinkers belong to a third group, and they are fascinated by the way that AI systems can cast light on the human mind. Some in this category are only peripherally concerned with the AI systems themselves. Margaret Boden, a central figure in the UK AI community, was quoted in 1992 in the New Scientist, as saying ‘I don’t give a damn about computers. I’m interested in the human mind, not computers…I think I would probably refer to myself now as a “computational psychologist”’.4 This is a fine illustration of category-3 process-thinking.


In contrast with process-thinkers, outcome-thinkers focus on the output of AI systems—the social consequences, the economic impact, the practical uses, the regulatory challenges, the business results, the ethical implications, the delivery of working systems, and more. Under this heading come policymakers, business people, ethicists, lawyers, economists, as well as all sorts of users of technology and AI.


This distinction between process-thinking and outcome-thinking helps us make sense of the divide between Chomsky and Kissinger. Chomsky is largely a process-thinker, while Kissinger is predominantly an outcome-thinker. In Chomsky’s view, the processes underlying ChatGPT are uninteresting. He is an expert on how humans acquire language capabilities and argues that this human mechanism is superior beyond words to the processes underlying ChatGPT. Chomsky is a category-2 process-thinker, and he concludes, broadly, that the human mind is immeasurably more fascinating and capable than AI systems; indeed much too complex and sophisticated to be modelled by machine. He is, on available evidence, not an outcome-thinker. If he were, he would surely at least doff his hat to the current performance of AI systems. He would perhaps note their great potential in, say, medical diagnostics, treatment, and disease prevention, or indeed their profound threat to humanity if, for example, AI is weaponized in the wrong hands.


Kissinger, in stark contrast, is focused on impact and implications. He is not an expert on the inner workings of machines or minds, but he can see the impact AI might have. For Kissinger, what these systems do is of paramount concern, rather than how they do it.


Chomsky and Kissinger are extreme case studies in process-thinking and outcome-thinking. Most people working on AI are a mix of both. But people often lapse into one variant of process-thinking when they question the potential of AI.


In the 1980s, the bible of aspiring AI gurus was the Pulitzer prize-winner Gödel, Escher, Bach by Douglas Hofstadter.5 In that book and elsewhere, Hofstadter showed himself to be one of the great thinkers on AI, which is why I was astonished in early 2018 to read his observations on machine translation of languages:


I am not in the least eager to see human translators replaced by inanimate machines. Indeed, the idea frightens and revolts me. To my mind, translation is an incredibly subtle art that draws constantly on one’s many years of life experience, and on one’s creative imagination. If, some ‘fine’ day, human translators were to become relics of the past, my respect for the human mind would be profoundly shaken, and the shock would leave me reeling with terrible confusion and immense, permanent sadness.6


If Hofstadter is referring here to automatic, real-time, translation that might enable humans in different countries to communicate freely on day-to-day matters, I do not share his revulsion. This advance would be of immense social and economic benefit. He may instead be contemplating the death of the great art of translating, say, the ancient Greek philosophers into English or perhaps the art of converting the finest Russian prose into wonderful French. But in either event, notice the strength of feeling and the steely focus on process rather than outcome. This kind of process-thinking is not unusual among some of the early giants of AI, especially those who were and remain sceptical about its potential.


Contrast the purism of the process-thinker with the pragmatism of some outcome-thinkers. Two fine examples here are the erstwhile political opponents Tony Blair and William Hague. An unlikely duo, perhaps, but they joined forces in 2023 to write a compelling report, ‘A New National Purpose: AI Promises a World-Leading Future of Britain’. There they say that AI:


is the most important technology of our generation.…The potential opportunities are vast: to change the shape of the state, the nature of science and augment the abilities of citizens.…But the risks are also profound and the time to shape this technology positively is now.7


More, referring to the international stage, they argue that ‘the nations that can effectively reshape their states around technology will be the ones to define the future’.


Another powerful example of outcome-thinking comes from Bill Gates, a man who can comfortably switch to world-class process-thinking when the circumstances require. He describes a demonstration of a version of GPT as ‘revolutionary’ and that the ‘whole experience was stunning’. He went on to predict that AI ‘will change the way people work, learn, travel, get health care, and communicate with each other. Entire industries will reorient around it. Businesses will distinguish themselves by how well they use it.’ And he also describes how AI ‘can reduce some of the world’s worst inequities’, especially relating to health, education, and climate change.8


It is hard to believe Hofstadter and Gates are talking about the same phenomenon. They diverge, as with Chomsky and Kissinger, because they are looking at AI from different perspectives—process-thinking and outcome-thinking.


# Reflections
When I was working on my doctorate in AI and law, I was a fully paid-up member of the process-thinker camp. My focus was largely on the ways that lawyers and judges solve problems and whether their lines of reasoning might be replicated in that early type of AI system known as an expert system. As a process-thinker, I agonized, for example, over the way that legal knowledge could be represented and stored in computer systems and the way that these systems could draw legal conclusions. Was ‘propositional logic’ a sufficiently rich formalism or did we need to use ‘first-order predicate calculus’ or even some form of ‘deontic logic’? I really did talk like that. These were arcane but, I felt, important questions of process that absorbed me for years.


Later that decade, though, when I led an AI initiative in one of the giant international accountancy and consulting firms, I shifted perspective and became preoccupied with the outcomes of these systems. We needed a tool that could help general auditors to assess the VAT affairs of companies. We wanted a system that could indicate when a legal action could no longer be raised because too much time had passed. These were practical questions about outcomes that mattered a lot. The users of our systems were patently uninterested in whether our system relied on propositional or deontic logic. Now a fully fledged outcome-thinker, I came to define our group’s mission as ‘the use of computer technology to make scarce expertise and knowledge more widely available and easily accessible’. This definition was pragmatic rather than purist, and indifferent to the techniques we used.


Although in my current advisory work I remain largely outcome-focused, I nonetheless continue to be fascinated by the full range of process issues that underpin AI, especially the philosophical, psychological, and technological questions. But I am conscious that process-thinking and outcome-thinking are very different mindsets rooted in very different cultures. To ignore or conflate the two can lead to all sorts of confusions, misapprehensions, and errors, to the more egregious of which I now turn.
