# Chapter 4
# Confusions
When Chomsky and others apparently trivialize artificial intelligence, they disregard the great benefits and dangers of future AI. When Kissinger and like-minded commentators make bold, sweeping claims, they make light of the massive technological advances, cultural shifts, and legal changes that will be needed if their predictions are to become reality. But the problems that flow from these very different views of AI extend well beyond two eloquent public intellectuals talking past each other in op-eds.


# Muddled Thinking about the Threat of AI
In the first instance, AI experts who make wildly different claims and predictions are causing great confusion in society. What is the average mortal to think when ostensibly expert people take diametrically opposed positions and do so with equal passion, conviction, and apparent insight?


Are we all doomed and hurtling towards some kind of dystopia in which humans are obliterated or, at best, retained on earth at the whim or discretion of massively capable AI systems? Or is this just a more complex repeat of the kind of furore engendered by the spinning loom and the combine harvester—machines that in the end simply enhanced our lot?


I discuss the substance of AI’s existential threat to humanity in Chapter 8. For now, I want to give further examples of the way public discussion of this issue can be muddled. Take the popular British podcast ‘The Rest is Politics’ hosted by Alastair Campbell and Rory Stewart. This podcast enjoys millions of downloads, and so many people will have heard an episode in mid-2023 in which the Nobel laureate Sir Paul Nurse said that ‘AI has been overdone.’ He went on to suggest that ‘part of the problem is the name…let’s start calling it “machine learning” and then we don’t scare the shits out of everybody’.1 A couple of weeks later, in the same podcast series, the bestselling historian Yuval Harari took a very different stance, suggesting recent advances in AI were ‘an extremely dangerous development’ and warning of the possibility of ‘new types of totalitarian regimes’2 and even spoke of ‘the end of human history…history will continue but with someone else [AI] in the driver’s seat’.3


Again, I ask, what is the non-AI person to make of this tension? And again I say that we are seeing here the difference in perspective and culture of process-thinkers and outcome-thinkers. Harari is preoccupied with the impact of AI on humanity. The reaction of Sir Paul, a pre-eminent scientist, is a case study in process-thinking. To suggest that the risks arising from AI would somehow dissolve if people understood that these systems simply capture and crunch data is to neglect the massive impact that machine learning systems can actually have. It might as well be said that computers cannot bring about harm because, in the end, they are just all about 1s and 0s. Sir Paul may feel—and here I speculate—that machine learning systems are crude constructs when compared with the human biology in which he is so deeply expert. This would be a little like Chomsky’s argument when he insists that computers are manifestly inferior to the human mind in language acquisition. They are not wrong, but this kind of category-2 process-thinking can encourage people to ignore the great practical consequences of existing and future AI systems.


Sir Paul Nurse is not alone. For example, Michael Wooldridge, an Oxford professor of computer science, in his first-rate Turing lecture in 2023, in riffing about ChatGPT and generative AI, said that ‘you are not interacting with a mind. It is not thinking about what to say next…it really is just a glorified version of your autocomplete…on your smartphone.’4 I have heard this particular piece of category-1 process-thinking informally cited. I hazard that Wooldridge and others speak in this way to offer some reassurance that AI systems are not in possession of anything like human minds. But in downplaying the underlying process, I contend we can obscure the reality that these mindless systems can undertake a wide variety of tasks at human level and beyond.


Another confusion in AI conversation crystallized for me when Daniel Susskind and I were interviewing a wide range of professional workers in preparation of our book The Future of the Professions (2015).5 Time and again, we noted that professionals see much greater scope for AI in disciplines other than their own. Doctors are quick to suggest that AI has great potential in law, accounting, and architecture, but instinctively they tend to resist its deployment in healthcare. Lawyers assert confidently that audit, journalism, and management consulting are ripe for displacement but offer special pleadings on its very limited suitability in the practice of law and the administration of justice.


Even within individual professions such as law, corporate lawyers deny that their work is susceptible to change. They point to, say, property lawyers, suggesting that a revolution there is long overdue. Similarly, in medicine, general practitioners will maintain that the old way of providing primary healthcare is pretty much the only way. But they will pick out, say, radiologists and suggest that these colleagues are, in the end, image processors and so crying out for revolution.


Many, if not most, professional workers regard themselves as artists in their own craft. They look upon their work as the very embodiment of what machines will never be able to do. All manner of biases and dissonances are going on here, but the undeniable fact is that the professions and the white-collar workforce are overflowing with people who believe that AI has massive potential but ‘not for us’.


This ‘not-us thinking’ is understandable, but it is often incoherent and indefensible. Professionals have invested themselves heavily in their work—long years of education, training, and plain hard graft. Their status and often their wealth are inextricably linked to their craft. And they are so steeped in traditional methods that they cannot imagine a credible alternative to the way they work. They cannot conceive a paradigm different from the one in which they operate. Not-us thinking betrays, therefore, a psychological and cultural conservatism. But it is often also coupled with an authentic moral commitment to long-settled ways of working. Lurking there too, perhaps, is a fear of ‘technological unemployment’ (workers being replaced by machines—see Chapter 8).


I had an out-of-body experience with a high-powered group of actuaries some years ago that epitomized not-us thinking. I set out my stall about advances in AI and was told in peremptory terms that they saw very little of relevance for them in what I’d said. I responded that I could scarcely imagine a profession whose work was more susceptible to AI. But I made no headway that day. It’s extraordinary the lengths to which we go to convince ourselves of our own indispensability.


Invariably, not-us thinking is also a form process-thinking. Here’s the nub—people don’t like a threat to a human process that they value, especially one in which they have invested so much of themselves and take a pride. But it transpires that the process itself is not of great interest to the recipients of professional service. Patients don’t want doctors; they want health. Nor do they want therapists; they want peace of mind. Clients don’t want architects; they want safe, functional, attractive buildings. Nor do they want court-based dispute resolution; they want to avoid legal problems in the first place. When the trappings are demystified and the traditions are subjected to scrutiny, it often turns out that little about the way professionals currently work—their process—is intrinsically important—that is, valuable in and of itself. The importance instead lies in the outcomes and the results attained and the value and benefits secured. Customers don’t want power drills; they want holes in walls.


It is remarkably confronting for all of us to ask what aspects of our work are intrinsically valuable, worth engaging in—meaningful—for their own sake, because much of our time is spent on activities that are simply of instrumental value. This means their significance lies only in the outcomes they deliver, and so they may well be vulnerable to AI.


# AI and Professional Work
With all of this in mind, I have naturally given much thought to the impact of AI on my own work. Not long after the launch of ChapGPT, I conducted a modest experiment. Wearing one hat, I have written many columns for The Times newspaper. When asked in early 2023 to write a piece on the impact of AI on lawyers, I asked ChatGPT (version 3.5) to draft 800 words on this very subject—for The Times, please, in the style and drawing on the writings of Richard Susskind. I cast a patronizing eye over the column that had stuttered out in seconds. Not bad at all, but in classic not-us thinking mode, I concluded it was not a patch on the real thing. Then I invited the recently launched GPT-4 to have a go. And that was when a shiver crept up my spine. It was a level up, no question—the product really could have been an early draft of my own. My immediate thought was this: there will be no need for me as a columnist when, say, GPT-6 or GPT-7 is available. I also thought that a better prompt would be to ask for an 800-word synthesized view of the world’s top-twenty leading experts on AI and law and written in the style of a proper author like Margaret Atwood. I encourage everyone to ask what their discipline will be like when we have GPT-6 or GPT-7. (This is a form of ‘what-if-AGI?’ thinking.)


My favourite management writer Ted Levitt encourages us to question the common ‘belief that there is no competitive substitute’ for an industry’s ‘major product’.6 We must, instead, be open to the idea that we might well be dispensable, that what we do, the services and outputs that we provide today, might be delivered quite differently. And if, for example, entrepreneurial start-ups find radically new tools or methods for meeting consumers’ needs or clients’ demands, then, as I say ceaselessly to my clients, neither the market nor people who cannot remember a pre-AI world will show loyalty to traditional working practices.


All of that said, I believe that most of the short-term claims currently being made by commentators about AI in the professions and white-collar work hugely overstate its likely impact. More significantly, however, I also think that most of the long-term claims hugely understate its impact. AI will not transform society and economic life within the next two to three years, but it will begin to do so, in my view, in the late 2020s and beyond. There is much to be done in the meantime but the change for professionals and white-collar workers will be incremental rather than in one big bang.


In the short term, in my view, the main impact on much work will be in automating what people already do, bringing improvements in efficiency and productivity. In the medium term, we will see the boundaries between traditional professions break down as AI enables multidisciplinary service. In the long term, the main competition for professional firms will be AI-empowered clients, organizations that can do much for themselves without referring work to external experts. Pay heed, professionals—the competition that kills you won’t look like you.


# AI Fallacy
When I follow this line of thinking with professionals, they often respond by saying something like, ‘Now hold on a minute. I think, I reason, I’m innovative, I’m thoughtful, I’m affable, whereas a machine is none of these, and so…what I do is safe from AI.’ This kind of not-us thinking commits what Daniel Susskind and I call the ‘AI Fallacy’—the mistaken assumption that the only way to get machines to perform at the level of the best humans is somehow to replicate the way that humans work.7 In denying that AI can ever take on aspects of their work, most people focus on how they go about their work—the current process—and claim that a machine cannot and could never work as they work. This may or may not be true. But it misses the much bigger point that machines don’t need to copy us to deliver the outcomes or outputs that customers, clients, and users want from their providers.


The error is failing to recognize that AI systems do not mimic or replicate human reasoning. To think otherwise is to take a much too human-centric view of AI. We find it hard to do otherwise. Here we see Blake’s ‘mind-forg’d manacles’ in play.


Consider autonomous vehicles. I don’t believe anyone seriously suggests that the optimum way to advance in that field is to develop robots to sit in the driving seats of conventional cars, mimicking the way that human beings drive. But that is the equivalent of envisaging robots in hospitals, courtrooms, or offices, or as some kind of substitute for the human professional. Rather, our increasingly capable systems will operate in the broad world of human professionals by engaging in tasks that take advantage of their unique capabilities and not ours. This was clear in 1997, when Deep Blue beat Garry Kasparov, and in 2016, when AlphaGo vanquished Lee Sedol. These humans were outgunned by remarkable algorithms operating on mammoth bodies of data, powered by vast processing power. They were not beaten by machines that were designed to copy the working processes of these masters.


One further illustration of the AI Fallacy pops up in discussions about the ‘transparency’ of AI systems. A little background is needed. Expert systems, the first generation of AI systems, were able explain their lines of reasoning. They did this by showing the paths that were followed along their decision trees. Second-generation AI systems based on machine learning cannot explain their output in an analogous way. There is no simple deductive path to follow. Their operations are far more opaque and, as Wolfram notes, often beyond our grasp. Generative AI systems do not reason like humans.


Many commentators and experts find this intolerable. If we rely on AI systems in critical circumstances, we should be able to peer under the bonnet to see what’s going on. Humans can explain what they do, so it is said, and so should machines. This call for a replication of human performance often falls foul of the AI fallacy because it asks for AI systems to operate like humans.


There are complex, much-discussed issues here.8 I have two thoughts to add to the debate. First of all, in daily life, we don’t ask for explanations for many of the pervasive and often critical systems on which we rely—our watches and our calculators, for example. We have confidence that they were developed responsibly and tested thoroughly (more of which in Chapter 9) and that the development and testing methods are open to scrutiny. Next, we shouldn’t ask more of our systems than we get from human beings. Take judges and doctors. In different ways, when these professionals come to decisions, we feel entitled to hear their explanations. But the nature of these explanations is far from clear. No matter how clever, no judge or doctor can provide an account of the neuropsychology or neurophysiology underlying their determinations. Even the deepest introspection by the brightest and most self-aware individuals cannot yield that level of explanation. Instead, they offer accounts that are better regarded as rationalizations—coherent renditions of premises and logic that are consistent with their conclusions. But these may well not be faithful even to the reasoning processes of which they were conscious. Judges may give ostensible reasons for decisions rather than actual ones, while doctors provide explanations for patients in the form of broadly intelligible discussions about symptoms, names of disorders, and the connections between the two, but rarely do they communicate in terms of the detailed underlying science. And we are happy enough with that.


Perhaps, then, what we need is not to mimic the human process of explaining but to have assurances on development methods and testing, coupled with AI-generated rationalizations that are consistent with but not directly derived from the way that AI systems do what they do.


# What Machines Cannot Do
OK, but when all is said and done, it is often objected, surely there just are some things that machines can’t do? For long, human beings have indeed been keen to identify the definitive limitations of AI, often by picking out tasks that humans can do but machines apparently cannot. The motivations here vary. We need to disentangle those who are making a moral claim and are really saying the machines ought not to be used in this way or that from those who are making a technical claim, suggesting it is not technically possible for a system to perform a particular task. And, of course, we should watch for others who are simply lapsing into not-us thinking.


Typically, those who make claims of technical impossibility assert that machines cannot, for example, be creative, conscious, empathetic, curious, brave, and intimate; they cannot exercise judgement or distinguish between right or wrong, or be merciful, or indeed be in love; they can’t achieve the genius of Einstein. AI sceptics enjoy adding to the list of capabilities that they cannot imagine of machines.


In one sense, all these claims are true, but trivially so, because these faculties and abilities might be said to be distinctively human characteristics and are precisely what distinguishes us, very roughly speaking, from plants, animals, and machines. They are part of the human toolbox of capacities—human processes. They are at the heart of how we conduct our daily lives.


However, it doesn’t really take us very far in our thinking about artificial intelligence to claim that AI systems are not, for example, creative in the way that humans are. For sure, it tells us that humans and machines operate differently—one through creativity, the other (today at least) via generative AI. But it doesn’t answer the practical question about the relative outcomes that each can deliver. An AI system might not craft original prose in the same way as a human author, but it can produce text in a configuration that has never existed before.


Many of these debates over what AI systems cannot do are, therefore, hindered by a failure to appreciate, once again, the difference between process-thinking and outcome-thinking, or sometimes by a conflation of the two. From a process-thinking point of view, to say that a machine cannot do x, y, or z is to identify ways of functioning that machines cannot replicate. It is to point to how humans operate and claim that AI systems cannot copy this. This may seem self-evident in that it is simply an assertion that AI systems are not biological humans, despite the widespread use of human-sounding terms like ‘neural networks’. Although the current wave of systems has indeed been inspired to some extent by the apparent neurophysiology of humans, this term, for now at least, is largely metaphorical.


A rich history of AI scepticism thus falls away. Take an early and compelling critic of AI—a philosophy professor by the name of Hubert Dreyfuss. In 1972, he wrote a book, What Computers Can’t Do, and he followed this up in 1992, with a title that I think even good old-fashioned AI could have generated—What Computers Still Can’t Do.9 The ‘critique of artificial reason’ offered by Dreyfus, however, is vintage category-2 process-thinking, rooted in a fixation on machines not working like humans. Interestingly, as machines have been able to do more and more, traditional AI thinkers—Dreyfus is one and Hofstadter is another—betray a deep regret, which suggests their preoccupation is as much with the desirability as the science of AI.


When we ask what machines cannot do, I say we should focus on outcomes. In Chapter 5, I look in this spirit at the possibility of AI-based judgement, empathy, and creativity. I leave for another day, another book perhaps, the question of whether AI systems will generate Einsteinian levels of insight and originality. My strong instinct is that they will.10
