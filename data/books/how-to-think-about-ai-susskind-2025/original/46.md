# Chapter 7
# Radical Structural Change
The distinction between automation, transformation, and elimination is of considerable practical significance for organizations that plan to bring about radical change through AI. In whatever sectors or markets they function, whether in the public sector or in business, one of the main challenges they will confront is embedded in the metaphorical question ‘How do you change the wheel on a moving car?’


The reality is that most substantial organizations that are contemplating the introduction of game-changing AI cannot simply press pause on their daily operations while they conceive and execute root-and-branch upheaval. There are no sabbaticals available for major businesses and public institutions during which they can reconceptualize and fundamentally reshape their activities. While its AI systems are being designed and introduced, no business can discontinue trading, no service provider can cease operations, no government could contemplate calling a halt to its daily provision. On the contrary, AI somehow has to be introduced while the mainstream operations are kept up and running.


# Transformation
Over the last couple of decades, countless businesses and public bodies have tried to transform themselves or innovate radically by introducing new technologies. However, no matter how radical or transformative their initial aspirations, most transformation initiatives end up as efficiency projects. This is because the only technological change that can realistically be imposed on a going concern is automation. New systems can be grafted onto current operations or they can replace current processes and tasks. But if the intention is to innovate or eliminate in the specific senses discussed in the last chapter, then this will rarely be achieved within the confines of existing structures. Very few of the most impactful recent systems have sprouted from the top of existing businesses. Instead, they have sprung up on the edge as start-ups or taken wing as spin-offs.


Generally, then, the answer to the question ‘How do you change a wheel on a moving car?’ is that you cannot (although I accept that on YouTube there are daredevils who demonstrate otherwise). But what you can do, to force the metaphor, is build and run a new vehicle that embodies and introduces innovative and eliminative technology, and you can then run the old and the new in parallel, and, over time, transfer the passengers from the old to the new.


In the context of AI, by way of illustration, some leading professional firms—lawyers, accountants, and consultants—are now recognizing what this will mean for them in the long run. They see that their main competition in the years ahead will not be with one another. Instead they will be vying with AI-empowered organizations that will be able to undertake a wide range of tasks and activities without engaging professional advisers. Or AI businesses may develop systems that eliminate the need for professional work altogether.


To survive, the more enlightened firms have grasped the notion that they themselves will have to build the systems, mainly AI systems, that will replace their old ways of working. Dispute resolution, audit, and tax planning, for example, will not be delivered indefinitely by flesh-and-blood experts. Massively capable systems will displace humans here and elsewhere. And it is dawning on firms that if they do not build the systems that replace them, then others certainly will. This self-disruption may seem like self-cannibalization, but if there’s going to be cannibalization, it’s best to be first to the feast (forgive me).


Astute leaders can see that this self-disruption cannot be brought about from within. They need to develop self-disruptive and self-destructive systems and services from entirely different structures that are nimbler, that are heavily populated by technologists, that are managed and capitalized quite differently from traditional firms, and that are focused on licensing products and solutions rather than charging for human service in six-minute units.


This is not a gentle hand on the tiller, suggesting a mild alteration of course. Rather, it is systemic, foundational, and radical change.


# Vision-Based Restructuring
The dilemma here is clear—if organizations aspire to deploying AI ambitiously in ways that extend beyond simply automating what they already do, then this cannot be done within the confines of their current structures and operations. The answer, again, is that they need to set up new vehicles or, in all likelihood, a whole fleet.


The same is true on a larger scale when you look at institutions such as court systems, universities, and health services. Much is currently being said about the ways that AI will transform our traditional public bodies, but the prevailing mindset and planning remain steadfastly focused on automation and task substitution rather than innovation, elimination, and radical change.


We will not make the most of AI in our justice, education, and health services by concentrating only on efficiency gains and on injecting AI into our current ways of working. The change process here cannot be legacy-driven, building layer upon layer of AI systems onto the shaky foundations of our dated and outmoded institutions. This would be to stagger backwards into the future.


Instead, we need to be vision-based. Extrapolating from the remarkable technologies we have today and bearing in mind dramatic likely advances in the years ahead, we have to envision a very different world: in law, for example, one of AI-based dispute resolution and AI-enabled self-representation supported by systems that can help people to understand and enforce their entitlements for themselves; in education, rich virtual learning environments combining the insights of the finest of teachers and delivered through personalized learning that is customized for each student or scholar; and in medicine, AI-based diagnostics and treatment planning and AI-enabled self-care.1


To imagine this new world, we need a different mindset. Turning back to my anecdote in Chapter 6 about neurosurgeons, I reflected after the conference that in asking me to answer the question ‘What is the future of neurosurgeons?’, the organizers had asked me the wrong question. To ask any ‘What is the future of X?’ type of question, it is assumed that X has a future. I don’t say this facetiously. It’s a leading question. It’s a legacy-based inquiry, insisting that X should figure in the response. If you inquire about the future of physicians, surgeons, teachers, professors, judges, and lawyers, for example, you are generally expecting that a modernized and automated version of the people in these professions will be central to the response. But as I argued at the event, the future of health care lies not in an AI version of what we have today but in entirely new approaches, namely preventative medicine and non-invasive therapy. This led me to conclude that a better question would have been ‘How in the future will we solve the problems to which neurosurgeons are our current best answer?’


When we are thinking in an open-minded way about the long-term future of our justice, education, and health services, we should not be starting with today’s arrangements—courts, lawyers, judges, lecture theatres, tutors, hospitals, doctors, and nurses. We should be asking ourselves, assuming the likely capabilities of our machines, how in the future we might tackle the problems to which these institutions and people are our current best answer. This is the heart of vision-based thinking. Not reversing into the future, constrained and contained by how we do things today, but inspired and empowered by the outcomes we can expect from emerging AI techniques and technologies.


# New Vehicles
Only once we have the vision, should we ask how we get there from here. We have to develop road maps to these very different places. We will not reach there by improving the current vehicles as they trundle along. Again, we will need to build new vehicles for the development and delivery of the systems, services, and visions to which we aspire.


Our old vehicles have largely evolved around the needs and constraints of the providers. The new vehicles will focus on users—AI-empowered users.


These new vehicles will bear little relation to the organizations that spawned them. The mother ship, to mix my metaphors, may well continue on its long preordained course, increasingly automated but not fundamentally changed through technology. Think of the English court system today, not much different in many respects from that of the late nineteenth century. Or the UK’s National Health Service, recognizable in operation and concept to the version founded in 1948. The new vehicles for the resolution and avoidance of legal disputes and the management and prevention of health problems will, in style and substance, be very different from traditional court and health services, fuelled by the idea and practice of innovation and elimination.


# Research and Development (R&D)
In justice, health, and elsewhere in all sectors (public, private, charitable), the new vehicles that I envisage will need to have heavy-duty R&D capabilities. Consider the most successful consumer electronic and pharmaceutical companies. They haven’t yet invented the products that will be delivering their revenues in five to ten years. To face this uncertainty they engage in a never-ending process of innovation, housed in their R&D functions. Amazon’s annual spend on R&D is $73.21 bn, Apple’s is $27.6 bn, while Roche’s is $14.72 bn.2 Siemens (for whom ‘Innovation is our capital’) has more than forty thousand people working on R&D. These capabilities, many more of whose ideas fail than succeed, are the lifeblood of the businesses. Without them, they would wither and die.


So too in so many fields that, historically, haven’t had to change, especially those that have valued tradition. In justice, health, education, transport, and most other aspects of crucial public service, whether delivered by the state or the private sector, it will not be enough to create start-ups for occasional one-off products or services. An ongoing R&D function will indeed be needed. The same will be so across the professions, whose fundamental ways of working have not changed for many decades. Where tradition had once been a central value and source of competitive advantage, many firms will need to innovate relentlessly to keep in business. Through creative and inventive research and development, major organizations, as a core part of their mission, will be innovating, eliminating, and often self-disrupting.


In summary, it’s easy to talk about using AI to transform organizations and their services. We can often see quite clearly how a new technique or technology might have a profound effect and bring about colossal change. But engineering radical change is not simply a question of ensuring technological feasibility. Sustainable and pervasive change will invariably require overhauled organizational structures, new business and operating models, a changed mindset, a refreshed culture, and different skills and processes in support of relentless innovation.
