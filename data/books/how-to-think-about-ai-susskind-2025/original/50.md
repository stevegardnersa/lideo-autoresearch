# Chapter 8
# Categories of Risk
I am generally upbeat about AI. I can clearly see the great promise of artificial intelligence as a means of improving the human condition and overcoming some of our gravest social and economic challenges. For example, I look around the world at our health services, justice systems, educational provision, and financial markets, and with each I see colossal problems to which I am confident AI could provide serviceable and sustainable solutions. I can also imagine AI yielding huge efficiency and productivity gains across countless other sectors and industries.


However, I am at the same time increasingly concerned and sometimes even scared by the actual and potential problems that might arise from AI. I turn to these in this chapter and to some responses in Chapter 9.


Note, first all of all, that it’s possible to be simultaneously optimistic and pessimistic about AI. It’s coherent to regard AI as a double-edged sword. This contrasts, however, with the position of many newcomers to AI—they seem to be allied to one of two camps, one that is indefatigably positive, the other relentlessly negative. A nuanced middle ground seems more attractive to me, one on which we can at least talk sensibly about building beneficial AI while keeping the major threats at bay. Whether the market and legislators will accommodate this common sense remains to be seen.


Meanwhile, we should at least be clear about the nature and scope of the risks and threats arising from AI.


# A Classification of Risks
Tomes have already been written on the risks of AI. Conferences on the threats seem to be convened hourly. Our newspapers and social media pulsate with commentary on the dangers of artificial intelligence. My purpose here is not to crack all the problems. Instead, I want to help readers to think systematically and in a balanced way about the worries that AI is engendering. ## Category 1: Existential Risks
For long, there have been fictionalized and cinematic renditions of various AI-induced Armageddon scenarios. More recent observations by heavy hitters such as Stephen Hawking, Bill Gates, and Elon Musk brought the existential threat of AI into the public conversation.1 To pick one and so to get a flavour, Hawking told the BBC in 2014 that ‘The development of full artificial intelligence could spell the end of the human race.’2


In the broadest of terms, the threat under the existential-risk heading is to humanity or civilization—that AI might be instrumental in bringing about the extinction of much of the biological human race or the irreversible collapse of social structures. I suggest there are three broad types of existential threat: weaponized AI, unintentionally devastating AI, and destructive autonomous AI.


Weaponized AI involves the profoundly aggressive use of AI by bad actors (such as aggressive states, rogue regimes, terrorist groups, cyber criminals, or disturbed loners). One example might be the use of some form of AI to design, produce, and deploy a highly lethal biological weapon.3


Unintentionally devastating AI refers to the possibility of a system accidentally leading to some humanity-destroying event. As the unintended outcome of some massively capable system, the damage wrought here would not have been within the contemplation of the humans who were involved in designing and developing the system. I pitch this risk at a fairly high level—we know that when we build complex systems, they often give rise not simply to unintended results but to consequences that we have not even conceived. It’s not a huge leap to imagine that the unintended consequences of massively capable AI systems working in isolation or in collaboration could be dire. That I am not giving illustrations is part of the point.


The notion of destructive autonomous AI perhaps peaked in popular culture in the Terminator movies. This seemingly more fanciful threat is that of the emergence of systems that, once launched, can operate independently of humans and might bring humanity-destroying results. The destruction may or may not have been intended by those who introduced the systems, and so there may be some overlap here with systems I call ‘unintentionally devastating’. Again, I don’t believe we have the vocabulary to capture what might happen here, but in a homely, metaphorical way, somehow these systems might have ‘minds’ of their own (I am more rigorous on the question of conscious machines in Chapter 10).


This contingency is at least conceivable if we think of systems that, first, can be set goals or might even be capable of setting their own goals; second, can independently execute these goals, in part by accessing and deploying physical and digital resources; and, third, can recursively self-replicate, self-improve, self-protect, self-organize, and—importantly—vie with one another. Each of these capabilities is to some extent being put in place by AI scientists today. They are birthing self-sufficient, evolving machines that—again metaphorically—have purpose, will, and intent, and the capacity to compete. If their goals turn out to be life-threatening goals, then the threat to humanity is clear, at least in concept.4


These three types of existential threat call to mind Marvin Minsky, the AI pioneer quoted earlier, who spoke about AI keeping humans around as household pets. Less dramatically, Geoffrey Hinton pithily observed that there are ‘very few examples of more intelligent things being controlled by less intelligent things’.5 Hinton is often informally referred to as the ‘Godfather of AI’. In mid-2023, he said ‘he experienced an “epiphany”…and quit Google to speak out about the risks’ of AI.6


## Category 2: Risks of Catastrophe
A second category of threat, less apocalyptic and falling short of the substantial or entire obliteration of humanity, arises when AI systems might cause loss of life, injury, damage, or turmoil that can be categorized as catastrophic. This could take many forms. AI systems might, for example directly or indirectly sabotage critical systems—our power sources, internet connectivity, water supply, payment systems, financial trading systems, air traffic control, pharmaceuticals, weaponry, emergency services, and more. We can easily envisage AI systems that infiltrate and wreak havoc with our infrastructure, essential services, and our national security.


Catastrophic threats can be divided into the same three categories as existential threats—weaponized AI, unintentionally devastating AI, and destructive autonomous AI. While the threats are not to all of humanity or civilization, nonetheless they would, if realized, disable everyday life for those affected.


## Category 3: Political Risks
Where the first two categories of threat relate largely to widespread death, injury, and physical destruction, my third category of risk is political. The issue here is potential detriment to the fabric of society. Jamie Susskind sets the agenda for this subject in his books Future Politics, first published in 2018, and The Digital Republic, which was originally released in 2022. He explains the context in these terms:


Politics in the twentieth century was dominated by a central question: how much of our collective life should be determined by the state, and what should be left to the market and civil society? For the generation now approaching political maturity, the debate will be different: to what extent should our lives be directed and controlled by powerful digital systems—and on what terms?7


The advances in AI since these books appeared have vindicated his concerns, the most worrying of which can be grouped for current purposes under three headings.


The first risks relate to power. The generic concern is that the technology itself (often insidiously) and those who control AI (often but not always consciously) can exert extensive control over humans’ choices, they can scrutinize our activities, and they can shape our perceptions. They control our choices by fixing in code the parameters set for much of our social and working lives (in our use of social media, for example). In so doing, they force our behaviour by constraining options. They scrutinize us by relentless surveillance, ‘by gathering and storing intimate details about us, and even predicting our behaviour before it happens’.8 And they shape our perceptions by being able ‘to filter what we know of the wider world, set the political agenda, guide our thinking, stoke our feelings, and mobilize our prejudices’.9


Crucially, this dominance is sustained currently by ‘unaccountable power’. The huge tech companies with their inscrutable AI systems and their iron grip on power, capital, data, processing have the influence and impact of nation states. However, that impact is not subject to controls akin to those that constrain governments and public bodies.10


The second set of political risks concerns our liberties and freedoms. These range from the prospect of draconian methods of policing and law enforcement to threats to our cherished traditional freedoms—of thought and conscience, of speech and expression, of assembly and movement. These freedoms are increasingly ‘entrusted to private tech firms’ through whose systems these liberties will soon be curtailed and exercised. Our freedom of speech, for example, is already to a large extent enabled and contained by the editorial policy of large unaccountable major platform providers, not least X (formerly Twitter), TikTok, and Instagram.


Another freedom, from interference or intrusion, might also be prejudiced by AI. More often framed as our right to privacy, this is our entitlement to maintain our own personal space—data about us, data created by us, and even the data that is us (genetic data)—free from public scrutiny. This is no doubt threatened by increasingly capable systems. The most extreme example of scrutiny is AI systems that might be able to read our minds (see my discussion of the brain/computer interface in Chapter 11).


Third are the risks of AI undermining democracy. Some of these threats are to the processes underlying democratic engagement. There is particular concern today, for example, about AI-generated ‘deepfakes’—video clips, audio recordings, and images which can be highly realistic but false representations of living people. When these people are politicians, fabricated versions of their views can be propagated convincingly and irretrievably, often virally, across social media. Equally, entirely fictitious online personae can be created, using fake accounts—plausible presences posing as politicians or expert commentators, disseminating false narratives.


At the same time, democratic discourse—public debate—can be subverted through what amounts to the manipulation of our thinking. Malicious AI-powered ‘bots’ (software that automatically performs certain, often repetitive tasks) can relentlessly spread misinformation and disinformation across social media and the web. These can be deployed to proliferate falsehoods, to magnify social unease and general misery, to foment discontent and distrust, and to precipitate hostile social action. In these ways and others, any form of government that is driven by the will of the people is inhibited or effectively disabled, because the people would no longer be able to make free and informed choices.


## Category 4: Socio-Economic Risks
My next category of threat encompasses the various adverse social and economic effects that AI might precipitate.


Top of the list in the catalogue of societal upheavals for many commentators is the idea that John Maynard Keynes anticipated in a classic paper on ‘technological unemployment’.11 The issue here, crudely put, is that technology will replace human jobs at a swifter rate than it creates new ones. Some say that Keynes was plain wrong in predicting this. Largely in light of the great recent leaps in AI, a growing body of observers maintain instead that his timing was out and that human jobs will indeed be displaced or replaced by machines but later than the great economist expected.


Daniel Susskind has written a book on this subject, A World Without Work, originally published in 2020.12 He draws an important distinction here. On the one hand, he suggests there is ‘frictional unemployment’ when technology replaces some workers but there is still work left for humans to do. Indeed, technology might have given rise to these new jobs. The problem here is that there are not enough skilled people to take on this new work. This dilemma, bluntly, is captured by the thought that few lorry drivers in their fifties (if and when replaced by autonomous vehicles) will retrain as software engineers. On the other hand, there is ‘structural unemployment’ when there is an absence of jobs, full stop. Either way, the threat is to employment, or at least reasonably paid employment, and in turn to people’s livelihood, self-esteem, stimulation, and often to the meaning and purpose in their individual lives as well as to the rhythm and flow of daily family life. The socio-economic fallout of large-scale frictional or structural unemployment, if this came about rapidly, would be incalculably disruptive to communities and their economies.


It might be conjectured or countered that the advent of massively capable AI systems will lead us towards an entirely new economic system, one in which these systems generate enormous increases in productivity, whose fruits in turn will be widely distributed across humanity. Advocates of this position speak confidently about the impending era of ‘abundance’,13 and even a surfeit of equitably allocated new-found wealth. Others, instead, see a major threat here. Far from everyone living happily ever after, they point to the undeniable current concentration of economic power enjoyed by a small number of technology companies (including Apple, Alphabet (Google), Amazon, Meta (Facebook), and Microsoft) and worry that these and future AI corporations will be less concerned with ensuring prosperity for all than with returns for their shareholders. Sure, AI may lead to a much larger economic pie, but so long as this new-found wealth is in the grip of listed or private companies, there’s no reason to expect an invisible hand will steer sufficient income to those who are no longer employed or employable. In short, the risk here is that AI will mean the wealthy will become wealthier, the poor will become poorer, and the disparity between rich and poor will increase radically. This could mean economic meltdown for most people, as well as a dehumanization of those who no longer feel they are contributing economically to society or their households.


Beyond the prospect of mounting disparity in income between the rich and poor, there is another looming risk of AI-induced inequality—between the AI-haves and the AI-have-nots. In the near future, to have access to AI systems and facilities will, effectively, mean access to superior services, such as health (for example, AI-based diagnostic systems) and educational provision (for instance, personalized learning). In the longer term, when AI enhances and extends the capabilities of humans (see Chapter 11), there is a threat of an ever greater digital divide than we have so far contemplated—a class distinction between those whose intellectual, sensory, and physical capacities have been boosted by AI and those who are digitally unenhanced.


Other social risks abound, because implementation of AI comes at a cost.


The environmental impact of AI is clearly a major concern. The computing resource required to drive the current wave of systems is enormously consumptive of power. Another worry is the collateral damage occasioned by the vast-scale gathering of data that feeds AI systems—for example, the danger of releasing confidential information.


And finally on the social risks, there is the wide gamut of deeply antisocial behaviours that we already endure on social media, which are likely to be magnified or generated directly by AI systems—content that is abusive and hateful, immoral and exploitative, threatening and bullying, defamatory and deceptive, as well as fraudulent and illegal. The harms already caused online are profoundly disturbing, especially when those affected are children. We also agitate about future generations being destined to solitary lives, staring at screens or immersed in virtual reality (although we might ponder that the reading of books is rarely frowned upon as being too solitary).


## Category 5: Risks of Unreliable Performance
Much less dramatically, there is a clutch of shortcomings with contemporary AI systems that means they are not always reliable. Worse, their output might be in various ways objectionable. I am in little doubt that most of today’s faults will be overcome in one way or another, although I expect that new ones will arise as novel techniques evolve. This is the way of technology—technological advance brings fresh benefits and fresh problems.


One of the main preoccupations of those who are currently calling for responsible AI is that today’s systems rely on huge bodies of past data that are likely to embody unacceptable bias—perpetuating, for example, racism, sexism, and misogyny. This bias is deeply troubling when it might underlie AI systems involved in, say, job selection, criminal profiling, credit checking, and offering insurance cover. We should be alive to the possibility of entirely new biases that may arise when synthetic data is generated.


There are concerns too about algorithmic bias—that the software that lies at the heart of the system was developed by a socio-economically limited cross section of engineers whose restricted world view will be likely to be reflected and perpetuated in the code that they cut.


As with all computer systems, AI systems will sometimes be faulty or defective in some way, which means that the systems can and will give rise to errors. Systems can also be subject to cyberattacks which can render them entirely unreliable or disable them entirely.


To combat these risks of unreliable performance, AI itself will bring solutions, but in so doing, new ones will no doubt emerge.


## Category 6: Risks of Reliance
My penultimate category of threat arises from our increasing and arguably excessive reliance on AI systems whose capabilities and performance are only dimly understood by most users. To be clear, it’s not always objectionable to rely on technologies without fully or even partially understanding what is going on in the bowels of the machine. It would be incapacitating if we always challenged and doubted the output of our wristwatches, Kindles, televisions, and countless other systems that are part of our daily lives. But when AI systems are used in critical situations, when their failure or errors could profoundly affect our well-being, then we should not yet rely on them unreflectively.


One major problem here is that, as noted in Chapter 2, we already do not understand why they do what they do in particular situations, and these systems are not capable of providing explanations for their output in the same way as humans. Take self-driving vehicles. On a recent visit to San Francisco, I took two journeys in autonomous cars, courtesy of Waymo. It has since become clear to me that most people have no idea that these cars are already fully operational. No longer an experiment confined to large car parks, they roam around almost all of the city of San Francisco. There’s no human in the driving seat to grab the controls in the event of a problem. Instead, the steering wheel moves as though the Invisible Man is there in the car and negotiating the traffic. I have written and spoken for years about self-driving cars. Sitting in one is a very different proposition. It’s hard as a lawyer not to reflect immediately on the liability implications of an accident. Most bracing, however, was the way the car steered its way round a sharp curve, a little faster than I would have been inclined given the unfenced sheer drop of 300 feet or so. With my wife and daughter in the car, I confess I wondered what on earth I was doing.


This is the kind of reliance I have in mind here, but on reflection, whether this reliance was excessive and unwarranted surely depends on the relative risk of some defect in the AI-mobile as compared with the chances of a human driver suffering from, say, a heart attack. Overall, I felt safe, but it may be that this feeling was not entirely justifiable. To come to an informed decision, which I am sure Waymo’s insurers have, a team of experts would need to pore through the system specifications, the development and testing methodology, the results of the tests, and comparative analysis of accidents by humans when driving.


We will increasingly come to rely on AI systems. Some of these will be stand-alone systems that will offer guidance on matters that are dear to us—our health, our wealth, and much else. More often, the AI components of systems that we depend on will be embedded and effectively hidden from us, whether, for example, as passengers in planes, purchasers online, and so forth.


Another issue here is our exposure when there is a failure of systems on which we rely deeply, a problem that is most acute when the systems are ‘innovative’ or ‘eliminative’, in the senses discussed in Chapter 6. This means that there can be no manual process to fall back on, precisely because the technology, at least according to my conception, has provided a facility that cannot be offered without the technology. Recall one of my illustrations: a failure of cash dispensers is not neutralized simply by putting human clerks behind the hole in the wall of the bank. Nor when on-board computers fail on spacecraft is this a wrinkle that can generally be ironed out by working things through by hand with pencil and paper. Failure of AI systems will lead to abandonment of journeys, projects, and services—or worse.


## Category 7: Risks of Inaction
Finally, in this catalogue of risks, there’s a threat that pulls in an entirely different direction. It flows from much of an optimistic nature that is said in this book. If I’m right and we already have the early tools that will evolve into correctives to, say, our increasingly broken health, justice, education, and finance systems, then we surely owe it to ourselves and future generations to embrace and develop these systems. The risk here, in myriad circumstances, is that of not using AI and, in turn, of missing exciting economic opportunities, of neglecting credible solutions to social problems, of failing to avert all manner of disasters, of letting chances for greater human happiness slip through our fingers.


Risk aversion brings its own risks. Preoccupation with the downside can lead to a culture of excessive caution. It is sometimes joked that in large organizations it is to their Risk and Legal functions that the best projects go to die. Defaulting towards restraint with AI will stifle innovation. In years to come, we’ll need to be bold in our deployment of AI if we are to secure the benefits. I say again that we must learn to balance the benefits of AI innovation—from medical diagnostics to autonomous vehicles—not just objectively against known dangers but relatively, by comparison with what we have today, namely inaccessible health services and flawed human drivers.


Our law and our world view will evolve accordingly. Lawyers will commonly ask under this heading in what circumstances it might, for example, be negligent not to use AI. Ethicists will point to moral obligations to embrace AI and not to fail to use artificial intelligence in given circumstances. Policymakers and strategists will claim that failure to deploy AI will render their organizations irrelevant or severely disadvantaged.


# Initial Reflections
The array of risks just outlined is a mountain range of threats. Those who are sanguine about the risks of AI have not thought deeply enough. We shouldn’t merely be uneasy or disoriented. We should be in a state of high alert. But not so much that we don’t force ourselves to glance briefly at the sun. We cannot look away or plunge our heads into the sand. There is too much to win and lose; too much at stake. We are creating massively capable systems that could bring untold benefits. However, these same technologies constitute, in a number of dimensions, a credible set of threats to our way of life and even to humanity and civilization. This is why I believe that balancing the benefits and threats of artificial intelligence—saving humanity with and from AI—is the defining challenge of our age.


We should be unequivocal about the enormity of the challenge that we face in confronting the potential threats of AI. Tackling each category of risk in isolation would of itself be a formidable enterprise. Addressing them in combination while coping also with the relentless emergence of new enabling technologies is a Sisyphean undertaking.


The risks themselves are diverse, by reference to the three standard dimensions of risk assessment—the likelihood of the risks being realized; the severity of the impact; and the timescales, short- or long-term. A full mapping of these three variables onto my seven categories of risk is a job for another day.


For now, instead, I offer my personal view on these risks. What keeps me awake at night? In my darkest hours, I cannot help but sometimes worry that we are creating a monster (Categories 1 and 2: Existential and Catastrophic). It agitates me how dismissive most people are about the possibility of technological unemployment (Category 4: Socio-Economic). I am concerned that governments will not be bold enough to confront the grave inequalities that could follow the successful deployment of AI (also Socio-Economic). And I am frustrated that we might miss the wonderful opportunities that AI could afford humanity (Category 7: Risks of Inaction). In the remainder of this chapter, I say more about each of these concerns, but it may be immediately apparent to readers that the risks that bother me most are not those that seem to be preoccupying most politicians and regulators.


## A Monster?
Are we building ourselves a monster? In my ruminations, I return too often to ten words from E. M. Forster’s short story, The Machine Stops: ‘man was dying, strangled in the garments he had woven.’14


One common school of thought, populated by policy and technology experts, holds that all this talk of existential risks and potential catastrophe is scaremongering and overblown. Their main argument runs that we have enough concrete short-term problems to occupy us (Categories 5–6: Risks of Unreliable Performance and of Reliance) that to be bothering with Armageddon or near-Armageddon is a distraction. This view is either disingenuous or plain daft. I have seen that it is a line of thinking that appeals to lay audiences. It is a reassuring and apparently down-to-earth position. But, by design or neglect, it obscures the undeniable fact that many significant AI systems are already available on an open-source basis and even the most expert computer scientists and technology specialists simply do not know how advanced AI systems will become and at what pace.


I would say that the mean view of AI experts, if we look a decade ahead, is that the humanity-threatening weaponization of AI is foreseeable but not likely and that the unintended destruction of civilization is conceivable but improbable. The likelihood of uncontrollable autonomous self-replicating machines is regarded as remote. But, again, we just do not know. What I struggle to understand is the doubters. In what other dimensions of human experience would we just shrug off a credible, even if not probable threat to humanity? We surely have to use our best endeavours to be prepared to divert the AI asteroid. As Kissinger writes:


Major countries should not wait for a crisis to initiate a dialogue about the implications—strategic, doctrinal, and moral—of these evolutions. If they do, their impact is likely to be irreversible. An international attempt to limit these risks is imperative.…For AI powers, pursuing some form of understanding and mutual restraint is critical.15


Kissinger’s focus here is on AI-related military threat, but the lesson is generalizable. I see it as irresponsible to focus today only on the immediate risks. The management of the short- and long-term threats should not be seen as alternatives. Nor should they be tackled sequentially. We need to work towards a comprehensive programme of risk management, handling each category in parallel and not one after the other. I am sure we have lessons to learn here from the military experience, not least in the international treaties, diplomacy, and realpolitik that brought some kind of global consensus in harnessing nuclear power.


I welcome the early global conversation that has begun on the existential and catastrophic risks of AI. The Bletchley Declaration that emerged from the AI Safety Summit is a good start.16 Likewise, reassuring work is being conducted at the UK’s AI Safety Institute and the US Artificial Intelligence Safety Institute.17


## Technological Unemployment
Regarding technological unemployment, after years of research across eight professions, we concluded in 2015, quite simply, that there would be much less for flesh-and-blood professionals and white-collar workers to do in years to come.18


Many observers summarily dismiss the notion of technological unemployment. Most of these doubters take a narrow view of AI’s impact on work, focusing only on automation (task automation), and failing to recognize the much greater significance of innovation and elimination. Some sit in the ‘not-us’ camp, dogmatically insisting that their work could never be undertaken by a machine (see Chapter 4). Others maintain that predictions of technological unemployment are not a new refrain. They have heard it all before and have concluded with some certitude that threats over the years of humans being replaced by new technologies have always proven to be overstated. Indeed, they often say, it’s more likely that all manner of new jobs will emerge and flourish on the back of the allegedly disruptive technologies. However, our research suggested that most of these new jobs are likely themselves to be gobbled by technology too. We found no evidence from our extensive study across the professions that any new jobs that arise (in these disciplines at least) will be ones for which humans are in the long run better suited than machines.


Beyond the professions, other people are anxious. In the movie and TV industry, for example, there has been high-profile industrial action in opposition to studios that are expected to use AI extensively to generate movies and programmes—taking on roles that historically have been taken on by paid actors, actresses, and screenwriters.19 An early example of the power of AI here is the systems that can already narrate books in the voices of, for example, Laurence Olivier and Judy Garland.20


Take autonomous vehicles as a case study that may be closer to home. It is hard to imagine that this innovation will not have colossal consequences for up to 10 per cent of the labour force, people whose work, directly or indirectly, involves or is dependent on cars and lorries—from drivers themselves to owners and staff of hotels in stop-off points, to the population of entire towns in the United States, for example, that were built to serve passing vehicles.


Very few politicians have looked squarely at the looming challenge of technological unemployment. It’s a problem, I suspect, they would like to leave to their successors or to the next government. The prospect of AI replacing workers is no doubt an issue that is placed by most policymakers on the too difficult pile. I may not have the answers here, but failing to discuss the issue honestly, publicly, systematically, and in anticipation of the likely problems (rather than reactively) is surely not the most responsible path to follow.


## Confronting Inequalities
Likewise, I worry that governments will fail to recognize and confront in time the grave inequalities that could follow from the successful deployment of AI. I see little serious energy being expended anywhere on frank public debate about how the state might require an unprecedented redistribution of wealth—colossal AI-originated wealth. Not least in the US, the prospect of inviting big tech companies to share their winnings with the populace will scare most legislators and leave the investment community queasy. The alternative, in time, will be unprecedented inequalities.


## Opportunities Missed
Finally, I really do fear that we might delay unnecessarily in enjoying the countless opportunities that AI could afford humans and communities worldwide. Remember, again, the great systems and technologies that have yet to be invented: it’s not fanciful to hope that these massively capable systems could generate unprecedented economic value and, subject to my comments above, thus alleviate poverty; or that many of our most challenging problems—from climate change to global health—will be ameliorated if not solved through AI systems; or that the expertise and creations of the few can be shared with the many.


But there are powerful countervailing forces that might pull us in different directions. It’s much easier to talk about and dedicate resources to the risks rather than to the opportunities. It’s tempting to succumb to technological myopia—that is, to misjudge the future potential of systems by fixating on today’s limitations—and so to be inclined to underinvest. Under the everyday pressures of keeping major organizations up and running, leaders tend to focus their energies on short-term wrinkles rather than long-term seismic shifts, kicking commitment to AI down the road. The ‘not-us’ lobby too will inhibit progress in many areas, insisting that AI systems cannot take on the work they do and discouraging us from trying.


Finally, in what I regard as the global rush to regulate, we run the risk of legally inhibiting and prohibiting greatly advantageous uses of AI. This brings us to the question of how to harness AI.
