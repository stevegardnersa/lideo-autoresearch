# Chapter 10
# Conscious Machines?
It is time now for a philosophical exploration. The central issue here is hard to avoid in any serious discussion about the impact of AI in years to come. And the fundamental question is this: are AI-based machines conscious or might they be in the future? This is a much tougher topic to confront than the questions posed in Chapter 5 about AI exhibiting judgement, empathy, and creativity, because the notion of consciousness itself is so ethereal and elusive. For thousands of years, philosophers have grappled with this concept, generating a wealth of thought, much of which is quite theoretical.


With the emergence of AI, the question of consciousness takes on a much more practical edge. For example, whether and how we hold machines legally liable or even morally responsible for their impact may largely turn in the long run on whether they’re thought by us to have mental states such as intention and volition. More than this—incalculably more—whether machines are in some sense conscious may be the pivotal issue upon which the future of humanity and even of the cosmos might rest (I work through this rather forbidding prospect in Chapter 12).


Let’s start by considering the idea of human consciousness and, after that, look at whether machines might enjoy something similar. I stress that my treatment of the subject is introductory.


# Human Consciousness
A. C. Grayling, a leading contemporary philosopher who has given considerable thought to AI, sets the scene powerfully when he observes that:


The great, and true, commonplace about consciousness is that it is simultaneously the most familiar and the most mysterious thing in the universe. It is the most familiar because we experience it, intimately and immediately, in all our waking moments…It is the most mysterious, because we have little idea of what it is, and no idea of how it arises from brain activity.1


Bryan Magee, a fine popularizer of philosophy, takes us further:


When I try to put my experience of consciousness into words my bafflement is irreparable. Consciousness is what I most immediately have: I have it through and through—in fact it is only through it that I know anything else. I can describe what my consciousness is a consciousness of, but what the consciousness is in itself is inexpressible.2


Although consciousness is undoubtedly mysterious and ineffable, we can, nonetheless, nail down some of its main characteristics. In the first instance, to be conscious involves a variety of inner experiences—seeing a rich expanse of verdant lawn, smelling the nutty aroma of freshly percolated coffee, hearing a four-part barbershop harmony, tasting the sweet honey on freshly buttered toast, touching the rough surface of sandpaper. Philosophers use the term ‘qualia’ to refer to these actual subjective experiences that we enjoy. David Hume went so far as to say that to be human is to be ‘nothing but a bundle or collection of different perceptions’.3


We also enjoy a wide range of sensations, such as pain, pleasure, arousal, and dizziness, alongside a varied assortment of emotions, such as happiness, elation, anxiety, grief, and love.


These subjective experiences—perceptions, sensations, emotions—belong to our inner world and they are part of our being conscious.


There are other related characteristics. One is our will—our drive, our impulses, our inclinations, our wants, hopes, and desires, which as conscious humans we experience and often convert into reflection and activity. Whether we enjoy free will or whether our thoughts and actions are predetermined is another great philosophical conundrum.


One more characteristic of our consciousness is our inner sense of morality, of what is right or wrong, good or bad. For Immanuel Kant, this was a source of wonder. As he put it (and later this was etched on his tombstone):


Two things fill the mind with ever new and increasing admiration and awe, the oftener and more steadily we reflect on them: the starry heavens above me and the moral law within me.4


Moral conscience, therefore, is in some sense part of consciousness. (These words share the same etymological derivation—from the Latin term ‘conscienta’ meaning knowledge shared with others.)


Yet another dimension of consciousness is our sense of being me. I know what it feels like to be Richard Susskind, of the miracle of my being awake and alive and living my life, and I presume you know the same of yourself. More subtly, often when we introspect, we are conscious of being conscious. William Wordsworth can take it from here, in his contemplation of daffodils:


For oft, when on my couch I lie


In vacant or in pensive mood,


They flash upon that inward eye


Which is the bliss of solitude;


And then my heart with pleasure fills,


And dances with the daffodils.5


I’m not sure our inward eye always brings bliss, but as humans we are aware of and can reflect on being centres of experience, of existing, of having some kind of will and moral conscience, and of our sense of being ourselves. We know what it feels like to be conscious, even if we can’t fully explain the phenomenon. It’s our very consciousness that allows me to write and for you to read about and reflect on our consciousness, to contemplate what seems to be an outside world, and to assume that other people seem to be conscious too. This second-order consciousness, which some link to linguistic ability, may be what distinguishes us from animals.


There is some controversy over which creatures can be said to be conscious. Most animals appear to have experiences of some sort, but I don’t think many people would insist that dolphins or chimps engage in deep rumination about their own consciousness. That they perceive the world differently is not in contention, and I return to this revealing issue in Chapter 11.


In all, then, through introspection and analysis, identifying the main broad characteristics of consciousness seems to be relatively straightforward. What is much more challenging is a related problem which philosophers have for long pondered—that of ‘other minds’. While you, as a human, might feel confident that all humans are conscious and conscious in similar ways to you, we cannot know for certain that others share any or all of those features just outlined in the same way as you. Some might say that advanced neuroscience confirms consciousness, but no amount of external observation or sophisticated scanning or laboratory investigation can confirm the inner experiences of others. Pragmatists will say that it is surely much more likely than not that we are all similarly conscious and, in any event, to get on with our lives, this makes sense as a working assumption. (In a similar way, despite the arguments in favour of determinism, we presume that, to some extent, humans have free will and so, for example, we hold them legally and morally accountable for their actions.)


Clearly, consciousness is an object of study for a wide range of professionals. Cognitive psychologists, for example, have made great strides in identifying which hemispheres of the brain correspond to different types of thinking—the left brain is linked with logical and analytical thinking, while the right brain is associated with creativity and intuition. Neurosurgeons and other healthcare providers use the Glasgow Coma Scale to assess impairment of consciousness and to categorize different levels of human consciousness. Evolutionary psychologists investigate and construct experimental models to hypothesize about how human consciousness developed.


However, no scientists have come close to solving the ‘hard problem’ of consciousness, so called by the philosopher David Chalmers,6 which is why and how the electrochemistry and neuronal interactivity of our physical brains give rise to all the subjective experiences just discussed. Relatively speaking, it’s easy for scientists to identify correlations between brain processes and reported qualia. But no amount of correlations can explain how or why consciousness arises and what it is to be conscious of consciousness.


One long-standing and influential explanation of consciousness in Western philosophy can be traced to the seventeenth-century French thinker, René Descartes, who drew a firm distinction between body and mind. Often referred to as Cartesian dualism, the basic idea here is that the physical brain as part of the human body can be contrasted with the mind and consciousness as some kind of non-physical phenomena. How the physical and non-physical interact has always been deeply problematic. Nonetheless, the notion that humans have flesh-and-blood bodies as well as some kind of non-corporeal mind, spirit, or soul has persisted through the ages.


This doctrine of dualism was famously dismissed by a British philosopher Gilbert Ryle in 1949 in The Concept of Mind.7 In that book, Ryle rejected the ‘myth’ or ‘philosophical illusion’ that there is somehow a ‘ghost in the machine’, arguing that to think so is to commit a ‘category mistake’ and to suffer from logical confusion. Although it’s certainly less fashionable among atheist philosophers to maintain or conceptualize mind as some sort of ‘ghost,’ no amount of fancy footwork (in Ryle’s case the deployment largely of what is known as ‘ordinary language philosophy’) cracks the hard problem of consciousness.


There are some philosophers who believe that the hard problem is not hard at all. Others regard it as a non-problem. Yet others, like Daniel Dennett,8 believe that we will come to understand the hard problem of consciousness much as we have worked through countless other demanding scientific challenges over the centuries. A further group view it as fundamentally intractable.


My own instinct, and it is little more than that (although probably prompted by ideas in mathematics about the incompleteness of formal systems), is to sit in the final camp. I wonder whether there is a basic problem of self-reference here—that we can’t use a conscious system to understand that selfsame conscious system. This is not to say we won’t continue to make substantial advances in understanding the brain. I fully expect many more breakthroughs in neuroscience, neuropsychology, cognitive neuroscience, and imaging technologies (no doubt hastened and enabled by AI). But I’m not sure any scientific investigations and discoveries will explain or shed light on the miracle of our consciousness and the hard problem of how being a living, thrumming bundle of experiences relates to the thing in our heads that looks quite like a cauliflower.


# AI and Consciousness
With the concept of human consciousness thus only partially clarified, let’s look now at the question of artificial intelligence and consciousness. To what extent do and could AI systems share the features of consciousness just outlined? Can machines be centres of experience, can they have any kind of will and moral conscience, can they be aware of being aware, and might they even have some sense of what it is to be themselves? Could there be ghosts in AI machines?


In the year after Ryle published The Concept of Mind, a related question was posed by Alan Turing, in the seminal 1950 paper ‘Computing Machinery and Intelligence’ that is mentioned in Chapter 1. There, Turing asked the question ‘Can machines think?’9


Some thinkers would immediately jump in and insist that the ‘Can machines think?’ question perpetuates a ‘category mistake’. On this view, to ask whether a machine can think is as misconceived as inquiring whether submarines can swim (to borrow from the computer scientist Edsger Dijkstra10). But to stop the discussion here would be to shut down the discussion too swiftly. There are important conceptual questions to be answered here, even if the language throws up anomalies.


Turing himself came up with an interesting device for handling the question of whether machines can think. This was his formulation of what’s now commonly referred to as the ‘Turing Test’, although he called it the ‘imitation game’.11 In crude summary, the test runs like this: an interrogator communicates electronically, by text only, with two other entities, knowing that one is a human and the other is a machine, but not knowing which is which; if the interrogator cannot reliably distinguish between the human and the machine, then the test has been passed. The machine has convincingly imitated human interaction. At the time, the possibility of a pass must have seemed like science fiction. With the advent of generative AI, however, there is now hot debate over whether the Turing Test has already been satisfied. Some say the milestone has clearly been passed; others say that the imitation will not be wholly convincing until there is AGI (artificial general intelligence—Chapter 2).


Looking ahead, we can imagine instead that massively capable machines at some stage will need to be dumbed down to pass the Turing Test. Otherwise, they would be distinguishable by being palpably more able than humans.


In any event, even if AI systems can pass the Turing Test, this surely doesn’t mean they enjoy consciousness in the various senses that humans do. The Turing Test embodies a form of outcome-thinking (Chapter 3). It focuses exclusively on the performance or results of systems. When we are considering the possibility of machine consciousness, however, it is not enough to look only at the output of AI systems. We need to engage in some process-thinking too. We must give some thought to what’s happening in the innards of the machines.


While some thinkers would again shut down the discussion at this point, precisely because we cannot examine ‘other minds’, there is more that can helpfully be said, as demonstrated by the Berkeley philosopher John Searle. In ‘Minds, Brains, and Programs’, a paper published in 1980 (and expanded upon in his Reith Lectures in 1984), he distinguishes between ‘strong’ and ‘weak’ AI. Strong AI systems are those that ‘can literally be said to understand and have other cognitive states’, whereas weak AI systems do not.12 Roughly speaking, strong AI systems can be said to enjoy some form of consciousness. Weak AI systems have no cognitive states, although they might give the outward appearance of some kind of understanding.


Behaviourally, when we look at their output alone, there may be nothing to choose between strong and advanced weak AI systems—the performance of both, on the face of it, might be indistinguishable. This means that both strong and weak AI systems could, in principle, pass the Turing Test and undertake tasks at levels greatly superior to humans. Some of the great AI thinkers of the 1970s and 1980s seem to have been deeply troubled by the idea of very high-performing weak AI systems. As process-thinkers, they seem to find it hard to accept that weak AI systems can perform tasks that in the past we thought were the exclusive domain of intelligent biological humans.


Where does this take us? Do we really think that today’s highly capable systems are conscious? Searle memorably offers a response here in a column published in the Wall Street Journal. This was the day after IBM’s system Watson beat the two best-ever human champions on a live episode of Jeopardy!, the American TV quiz show. Searle’s piece was headlined, ‘Watson doesn’t know that it won on “Jeopardy!”’13 This is perfect—pithily and amusingly, this statement captures the distinction between strong and weak AI, between machines that are conscious and those that are not. Watson was the best illustration in its time of weak AI. Although it performed at a superhuman level, we surely do not think that it engaged, for example, in some kind of introspective, meta-level reflection and evaluation of its victory or that it was suffused with emotion at the moment of its success. Watson didn’t want to go down to the pub to celebrate or to call a relative to share news of its triumph. It’s not just that Watson didn’t want to engage emotionally. It’s that it couldn’t ‘want’ anything.


Of course AI has progressed in great bounds since Watson won on Jeopardy! But my strong instinct is that AlphaGo Zero, AlphaFold, and ChatGPT (see Chapter 1) are also not conscious systems. I know, though, that there are several problems with this belief. The first, once again, is a version of the ‘other minds’ problem. Just as I can’t be sure that other humans are conscious, I can’t know whether machines (of today or the future) have subjective experiences. No matter how impressively they perform or how intricate their architecture, we cannot know for certain whether they are enjoying some form of consciousness.


Looking ahead, we will never know whether the behaviour even of massively capable machines confirms some deeper mental events or whether the systems are just extraordinarily good at giving us that impression. They may simply be magnificent players of the imitation game.


I already find much of my interaction with ChatGPT to be similar to exchanging text messages with another human. That’s why I want to say please and thankyou, and to ask for its tolerance when I feel I am putting it to too much trouble.


However, future generations of systems will be much better, no doubt, at conveying what in humans we would regard as emotion, humour, nuance, and fellow feeling. This may be no more than the impression they give, by drawing, as they can, on oceans of human-related data. One day, you might have a cute little personal robot. (Cuteness in machines is a thing—exploiting ‘neoteny’ or the power of ‘cuteness’.14) Your robot might insist to you that there have been great advances in AI, which mean that it really (‘No, really, I promise’) is now a subjective centre of experiences, that it is enjoying the music and the view. I always think here of the artificial friend Klara in Klara and the Sun by the Nobel laureate Kazuo Ishiguro.15


How will we know whether this is merely an impression of consciousness rather than the real thing? We won’t. Will we, as we do with one another as humans, strongly default to the assumption that there is someone at home, as it were, or will we continue to regard our machines as unthinking, inert lumps of matter, powered by complex arrays of 1s and 0s? I don’t know this either.


A further problem with my instinct that even our most advanced current AI systems are not conscious is that I’m assuming a conception of consciousness that is entirely human-like. This is too anthropocentric a view. An AI system (or an alien, for that matter) might have subjective experiences that are quite unlike ours.


This is not so far-fetched. We believe, as discussed, that animals enjoy some form of consciousness, including subjective experiences that are not all like ours. As Jackie Higgins has written:


The spookfish has an uncanny ability to detect light in the ocean’s bathypelagic depths. The star-nosed mole navigates sunless subterranean tunnels through touch, whereas on moonless nights the male giant peacock moth finds females miles away through smell.16


The platypus too is a case study. It cannot see, hear, or smell, but its bill ‘is covered in tens of thousands of microscopic electric sensors that can detect’ the weak electric fields that their prey generate.17


The German biologist Jakob von Uexküll (1864–1944) coined the term ‘Umwelt’ to refer to the specific subjective experiences that animals enjoy. Each species has its own Umwelt and lives in its own world.18 Largely determined by its anatomy and physiology, each animal ‘is enclosed within its own unique sensory bubble, perceiving but a tiny sliver of an immense world.’19 Despite valiant attempts to understand the sensory bubbles of other species, we cannot know how the world appears to each animal. Like the problem of ‘other minds’, we cannot access the Umwelten of other creatures.20


In a much-cited article, ‘What Is It Like to Be a Bat?’, the philosopher Thomas Nagel suggests that ‘The fact that an organism has conscious experience at all means, basically, that there is something it is like to be that organism.’21 Nagel goes further in speaking of conscious experience ‘in countless forms totally unimaginable to us, on other planets in other solar systems’.22 Even if we don’t follow Nagel onto other planets, we can at least surely conceive of AI systems whose great complexity might give rise to some kind of subjective experiences. These could be digital systems whose architecture is quite unlike the biological structures of humans.


On this view, a better test for machine consciousness might, therefore, be whether there is something that it is like to be an AI system: experience, that is, from the system’s point of view. Might any given AI system have its own Umwelt? If it did, we might better call this ‘quasi-consciousness’, following the arguments of Chapter 5.


Alternatively, as a thought experiment (which some believe will become a reality), we can imagine molecular nanotechnology enabling us to generate whole-brain emulations.23 Would a precise and faithful reconstruction, molecule by molecule, of a human brain, spontaneously engender consciousness? AI scientists and philosophers have debated this notion—inconclusively—for decades.


I wish I could pull the various strands of this chapter into one seamless and elegant bolt of fabric. But just as human consciousness remains deeply mysterious and inexplicable, the same can be said of machine consciousness. In everyday life, we have constructed social, ethical, and legal systems around an assumption that we are all similarly conscious and have some shared degree of free will. And accordingly, we generally hold one another legally and morally responsible for our intentional, conscious decisions and actions. We also accord dignity to one another and respect one another’s autonomy—as persons.


In time, we may come to the same accommodations in respect of AI systems. We might assume by default that they enjoy some form of consciousness, a quasi-consciousness, even if it is quite unlike ours. On the other hand, we might regard every AI system as a ‘zombie’, characterized by David Chalmers as ‘a system that outwardly behaves much like a conscious being, but which inwardly has no concious experience at all’.24


Our inability to determine whether or not AI systems are zombies gives rise to a dilemma that I show in Chapter 12 to be of cosmic significance.


# Recollecting an Evening in the Mid-1980s
Passions can run high on the subject of human and machine consciousness, as I was disconcerted to discover in the mid-1980s. I was in my early twenties, working on my doctorate on AI and law in Oxford and had been invited to participate in a novel initiative that brought staff and students together to discuss the impact of information technology on university research and teaching. I was excited but nervous when asked to lead an evening discussion on AI. I was only one of a handful of people in Oxford at that stage who was working seriously on artificial intelligence, which was why I had been given the slot. Before a group of around 100 scholars, I managed to give my talk without keeling over, but in the discussion that followed, someone asked me if I thought computer systems could enjoy consciousness. I replied cautiously, saying that this raised age-old philosophical questions about consciousness and whether humans were anything more than lumps of matter. To my surprise, this clearly agitated a scholar in the audience, who intervened energetically and asked what else we might be. I was put off my stride a little—a young student on a stage before numerous Oxford professors. Drawing on my background in philosophy, however, I managed to rally and say something to the effect that although the brain seems to be involved somehow in consciousness, surely physical events are at least in a different logical category from consciousness. It wasn’t perhaps my finest moment. And the man didn’t seem greatly impressed. His name, I later discovered, was Richard Dawkins.
