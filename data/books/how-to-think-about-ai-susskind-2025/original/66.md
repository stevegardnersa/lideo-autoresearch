# Chapter 11
# Coming Soon
AI is not the only technology in town. Numerous other digital advances will also transform our lives. Some of these are widely ventilated, such as quantum computing, robotics, molecular nanotechnology, 3D printing, blockchain, and the internet of things. Beyond digital technology, great life-changing strides are also being taken in other fields of science, especially in medicine, synthetic biology, and genetic engineering.


To conceive the future for humans solely in terms of AI is to miss the wider landscape, where all manner of existing, emerging, and not yet invented technologies converge, interact, and magnify one another’s impact.1


These are not the pages in which to tease out and then weave together the full set of technologies that will complement AI. I could wax digital about them all. Instead I focus on the two particular lines of development that fascinate me the most and will doubtless be of profound impact. The first stems from recent advances in BCI (brain–computer interface), and the second is the future development and deployment of VR (virtual reality). Alongside the many AI systems that I discuss in earlier chapters, BCI and VR may well help hasten a new epoch for humanity, enhancing and transforming human experience.


# Brain–Computer Interface
The main aim in the field of brain–computer interface (BCI) is to set up direct links between the brain and the computer. Although research in this area dates to the 1970s, it has lately been boosted by AI. Two breakthroughs in neuroscience and neurosurgery have fired my imagination.


The first is a system that analysed the brain signals of twenty-nine neurosurgical patients who were invited to listen to the same piece of music. Their neural activity—their intracranial electroencephalography data—was captured via a total of 2,668 electrodes that had been implanted on the patients’ cerebral cortexes (the surface of their brains) using a process known as electrocorticography. Then, a form of predictive modelling, known as regression-based decoding, was used to reconstruct and then generate the music that they had heard.2 You can hear it online. It is clearly (and spookily) identifiable as part of the 1979 song, ‘Another Brick in the Wall’ by Pink Floyd.3


I find it extraordinary that this system—very roughly—processed, predicted, and produced the music to which the patients were listening. It converted the recorded brain activity into a recognizable rendition of the song. This contribution to auditory neuroscience is not entirely novel. It complements earlier and ongoing work that uses computer models, brain scans, and electrodes to decode and reconstruct individual words and thoughts. Nonetheless, it is a dramatic case study.


The second breakthrough in BCI relates to a patient who was suffering from chronic tetraplegia—that is, paralysis affecting all limbs. A spinal cord injury had interrupted the communication between the brain and the neurons located in the part of the lumbosacral spinal cord that enables walking. The neurosurgeons re-established this communication by building and inserting a wireless ‘digital bridge’ between the brain and the spinal cord. This neurosurgical implantation of a brain–spine interface enabled the individual to stand and walk again on his own.


More technically, two cortical implants, sixty-four electrodes in all, were positioned on the sensorimotor cortex and in this way captured the electroencephalography signals. In real time and wirelessly, the system converted this data into predictions of motor intentions and in turn into epidural electrical stimulation commands that activated the zones of the lumbosacral spinal cord involved in the production of lower-limb movement.4


This kind of advance in BCI could radically improve the lives of many patients who suffer from related problems.


The commercial potential for BCI has also been recognized, most vocally perhaps by Elon Musk, who founded a company known as Neuralink in 2016. The mission of this business is to create ‘a generalized brain interface to restore autonomy to those with unmet medical needs today and unlock human potential tomorrow’.5 In other words, while the current focus is the development of implantable brain–computer interfaces to tackle disabling brain disorders, the apparent longer-term aim is that of enhancing humans.


This takes us into very different territory. It’s still early days in the field of BCI, but as the systems in this field become increasingly capable, it is conceivable and foreseeable that at some stage we will, to some extent, be able to direct our machines simply by thinking. Conversely, our machines will, to some extent, be able to read our minds. If we think we have privacy problems today, mind-reading machines will take us into a whole new realm. Our innermost thoughts, urges, and experiences might be monitored and evaluated.


More, it is seriously envisaged by some AI scientists that human brains and digital computers will eventually come together or merge, creating a new breed of transhuman. This is the central premise of the Singularity Hypothesis that is outlined in Chapter 2. When humans become digitally enhanced, cerebrally and prosthetically, in the words of Ray Kurzweil, ‘this technology will let us merge with the superintelligence we are creating, we will be essentially remaking ourselves…processing on a substrate millions of times faster than biological tissue, our minds will be empowered to grow exponentially’.6


Kurzweil’s vision is of massively extended human consciousness. Donald Hoffman, a cognitive scientist, seems to agree, suggesting that ‘AI can open new portals into consciousness, just as microscopes and telescopes open new vistas.’7 I am inspired in turn by the scientific writer Jackie Higgins, who has written in a different context that:


We see only a ten trillionth of the electromagnetic spectrum; imagine extending our range to perceive infrared heat like a rattlesnake or see ultraviolet light like a honeybee. Imagine experiencing the taste of a catfish, the touch of a star-nosed mole or the balance of a cheetah.8


A new era of transhumanism perhaps beckons, magnifying our intellectual capabilities, expanding our consciousness, while simultaneously reducing our dependency on our frail biology. Even if the chances of the deployment of BCI and AI are modest, the lesson here from Chapter 9 is that now is the time to think through the ethical and legal implications.


# Virtual Reality
While I write in the middle of a heatwave in this latest AI summer, another set of technologies, virtual reality (VR), seems to have headed into winter. After much hype, the metaverse, as VR is sometimes called, has not so far attracted the billions of visitors that some had expected. But it’s to this technology that I now turn. This is the second technological advance beyond AI that currently fascinates me.


The helicopter view is this—in years to come, I predict that human beings, liberated by AI from some daily toil, will spend more of their time in virtual reality than in what most would today call the ‘real world’. This may sound like a step too far, but I urge you to keep an open mind, remembering once again that our machines are becoming increasingly capable at an accelerating rate.


My virtual-reality epiphany came in late 1992, on an overnight transatlantic flight. At a bookshop in Philadelphia, I had found Virtual Reality, a newly published book by Howard Rheingold. This work kept me awake for the entire journey.9 It seemed to me, then and now, that when various technologies matured, virtual reality would bring fundamental change to daily human life.


I’m not going to try to define VR precisely. Jaron Lanier, one of the towering figures in the field, offers us fifty-two different definitions. This suggests that a classification exercise might not help us so much.10 Instead, I keep my description fairly loose and high-level. In general terms, then, most current VR involves users putting on purpose-built headsets and becoming immersed in computer-generated worlds. These might resemble planet earth (like flight simulators used to train pilots), or they may be fantasy realms (as in many online games). The aim is to create vivid online sensory experiences, including sight and sound, and sometimes even touch, smell, and taste as well.


While my early expectations of VR were shaped by reading around the subject, my current conviction of their great future significance comes in part from a series of personal experiences. I accept that what follows is more anecdote than social science, but take what I offer as a little evidence for my early hypothesis that humans in the future will indeed spend much, if not most of their time in some kind of virtual reality.


A few years after reading Rheingold, I caught an early glimpse of the future of flight simulation. I was working closely with Mark Saville, later a Justice of the UK Supreme Court. Lord Mark Saville was best known for conducting the Bloody Sunday Inquiry, for which, incidentally, he built a remarkable computer simulation of the events of the day.11 He was a keen pilot and enthusiastic about his flight simulation software. In his flat in the historic Temple in London, enabled by an uncommonly large monitor for the time, he flew us around London and—the highlight—under Tower Bridge. Even in that early iteration of flight simulation, it felt quite realistic. I recall ducking as we went below the iconic structure. A little later, over coffee, we confidently anticipated that simulators in years to come would be wildly better. A recent fully immersive trip in a VR-based system flown by one of my nephews confirmed this. None of this is news to online gamers.


Some years after, at a technology exhibition, a VR expert attached short rods of some sort to my right index finger and thumb. Wires led from the rods to a desktop computer. I was invited to put on a head-mounted display and was immediately immersed in a room in which there was a small table. A bowl sat on top, containing fruit, a billiard ball, and a tennis ball. I was encouraged to ‘pick up’ the balls. At first, I wasn’t sure what that meant. But I was told not to overthink. I reached my hand out gingerly and found myself squeezing each in turn. Although this was enabled by the small rods attached by wires to a machine, all of that was lost on me. The surface of the balls and their weight felt just like the real things. This was my first experience of ‘haptic’ technology, which gives users various sensations of touch.


Later still, this time at a legal technology event, I donned another headset and found myself in an elevator hurtling many floors up. The lift stopped, the doors opened, and there I was, perched on the edge of a building, hundreds of metres aloft, alone among countless skyscrapers. A wooden plank extended before me into mid-air, with sheer drops to the distant streets below. I was urged to walk out. Like half of the delegates at that meeting who had a go, I couldn’t bring myself to step forward. My visceral fear did not feel virtual.


In early 2024, I had an introductory demonstration of Apple’s Vision Pro headset and was bowled over by the lifelikeness of the 3D video; I then took a trip to East London to enjoy ABBA Voyager, the extraordinary concert given not by the artists but by digital versions of them; and in the Sphere in Las Vegas, in an immersive cinema experience, I felt the wind in my hair and the smell of citrus while wandering in an orange grove.


These experiences—flying under Tower Bridge, lifting billiard and tennis balls, walking the plank, Apple’s VR offering, applauding the avatars of ABBA, smelling the fruit—lift my expectations of the virtual realities that awaits us. I stress that most of these VR systems are the worst they are ever going to be. VR systems too are becoming increasingly capable.


Even before AI enjoyed its recent renaissance, there were already hundreds of millions of users of virtual reality. The majority are gamers, but others take educational courses, attend concerts, sight-see, and more. In years to come, and here’s the crunch, I expect that remarkable virtual worlds will themselves be generated by AI.


Living much of our lives virtually may seem grotesque to some readers. But we shouldn’t dismiss the idea so quickly. I would welcome a system that could give the elderly and disabled the opportunity to travel the world and, almost first-hand, to sight-see, and enjoy the sound, taste, smell, and touch of distant places. I myself would also like to marvel in experiences that are currently beyond my reach and ability—perhaps to hurtle through space or explore the beds of our deepest oceans.


At the same time, we shouldn’t fall into the trap of thinking about VR simply in terms of automation (see Chapter 6). It is common to find VR conceptualized as creating a lifelike environment which faithfully reflects the world around us. But this is much too limited. VR may afford us sensory experiences that are more intense than we enjoy in what we might currently call the ‘real world’. The colours might be richer, the sounds purer, the smells sweeter, the landscapes more sweeping than we have experienced before. Nor need virtual worlds be constrained by conventional physics. We might choose to be unfettered by gravity and human physiology. In virtual worlds, humans might soar like eagles, swim like dolphins, or roam worlds that bear no relation to the set-up given to us by mother nature.


We might also engage with the dead. We might be able to meet and interact with lost loved ones, recreated to a high degree of accuracy by generative AI. We might ask, ‘What would Dad have said?’ and an AI-generated father would appear before us, offering love, wisdom, and support. Whether we would want to, whether this would be healthy for us, and whether this would diminish or enhance our conceptions of mortality and humanity, I do not know. (Whether this should be deemed ethically unacceptable, I would not want to leave to technologists to determine—see Chapter 9).


More, and crucially, as humans increasingly commingle with the actual hardware (whether via contact lenses, nanobots, BCI, or not yet invented tools), the distinction between reality and VR that we experience will become further blurred. And if we ever extend our consciousness in the manner anticipated by the Singularity Hypothesis, then the distinction between real and virtual will dissolve altogether.


Bizarre though all of this might sound, remember that there is a weaker sense in which we already live much of our lives virtually. We may not, most of us, be wandering around wearing head-mounted displays. Nor do we look at the world as filtered through AI-enabled contact lenses. Not yet, at least. But, for good or ill, we do spend great chunks of time, peering into an online world, as though through a window, in which we socialize, work, trade, collaborate, research, and play. As our systems become increasingly capable, it won’t be a huge step to open the window and climb in.


It might be thought a matter of common sense that virtual reality is by its nature some form of secondary reality which resides one remove away from the real world that is itself surely the primary reality. But this view is open to challenge. On the one hand, if the virtual environment is the only space in which a given set of experiences can be enjoyed, where, for example, the online world bears no relation to our everyday world, then it doesn’t make sense to regard this as secondary. The virtual reality is the primary reality. Another challenge to the common-sense view is more profound and asks us to question the very nature of reality.


# Reality?
Focus now once again on virtual reality that does recreate the world as we know it. Imagine that our virtual experiences, increasingly generated by AI, become so vivid and realistic that they are indistinguishable from everyday life. Some profound and difficult questions arise. For me, the most mind-boggling is this—how do we know that our everyday experiences today are not themselves some kind of digital experience?


The idea that we might be living in a digital simulation is a central theme of one of Hollywood’s most celebrated science fiction movies, The Matrix (1999). The notion that we might be part of some massive digital apparatus has also been addressed by some serious thinkers. It’s a fine illustration of the way that AI can urge us to think deeply about—and even rethink—human experience and consciousness. The most extensive scholarly consideration of this topic can be found in Reality+, a fascinating book by the philosopher David Chalmers (who coined the phrase ‘hard problem’ of consciousness—see Chapter 10). In answer to the question ‘Are we in a simulation?’ Chalmers concludes, after rigorous and impressive argument, that ‘the chance we are sims is at least 25 percent or so’, which I find at once astonishing and deeply disconcerting. More importantly perhaps, he goes on to argue that ‘we can’t know we’re not in a simulation’.12


This position in some ways echoes a widely cited paper by another philosopher, Nick Bostrom. In ‘Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?’, Bostrom observes that:


If we are living in a simulation, then the cosmos that we are observing is just a tiny piece of the totality of physical existence. The physics in the universe where the computer is situated that is running the simulation may or may not resemble the physics of the world that we observe. While the world we see is in some sense ‘real’, it is not located at the fundamental level of reality.13


What should we take from this? The hard-nosed reader may well regard such talk about VR as fanciful claptrap. We have enough tsuris (Yiddish for trouble and distress, with thanks to both my late grandmothers) in the real world without concocting imaginary universes. I sympathize but disagree. On the one hand, as noted, there is and will be much value and enjoyment to be derived from hanging out in virtual reality. More philosophically, I am less convinced by arguments that are premised on a ‘common-sense’ conception of the real world. If we open our minds and probe into the concept of reality, we might come to doubt that the real world is the best place to be.


Start by imagining a powerful AI-generated VR system that gives rise to sensory experiences (‘qualia’) that again are indistinguishable from everyday sensations—say, the view of the lawn or the sound of the quartet. These sensations are somehow stimulated digitally in us by the VR system. Our sensory systems and brains convert the digital data into what feels like commonplace experience. Now take off the headset. Chalmers, Bostrom, and The Matrix urge us to ask how confident we would then be that the new sensations are themselves no longer in some kind of VR, induced perhaps by yet richer digital data—enabled by more advanced AI that is not apparent to us. Chalmers tell us we cannot know the answer here.


How you view this line of thinking will in part depend on how you regard what is variously called the ‘real world’, ‘objective reality’, the ‘physical world’, the ‘outside world’, the ‘external world’, or, simply, ‘reality’. Common sense might suggest it’s blindingly obvious that there is a material world, that we live in it, and that it exists independently of us.


For centuries, however, philosophers have challenged each element of this common-sense view. When I was an undergraduate, my philosophy professor convinced me that my kitchen at home only existed when I was actually there to perceive it. He started with smell. What could it mean to say there was a smell in my kitchen if there was no human there to experience the odour? The preconditions for smell, the trigger that somehow might give rise to the experience of smell, might be present but the actual sensation and experience of smell, the qualia, surely required a conscious human. Then he argued the same for sound—there might be something going on in the kitchen’s airwaves, but these ripples, he said, were not sound itself. Again, he said, to say there is a sound surely requires a human enjoying that experience. So it went on until the actual physical kitchen seemed to slip away, only existing when there was a person on site to apprehend the room. Now, this is all fairly standard stuff for any beginner in the field of epistemology and indeed for reflective people who debate whether a tree falling in a forest makes a sound.


The more I studied, however, the more the whole universe seemed to come tumbling down. I was intrigued by Plato’s allegory of the ‘cave’ in which humans sit, staring at the wall on which is cast a mere shadow of the world outside, a world that we cannot see directly.14 And I was unsettled by the opening few pages of Problems of Philosophy by Bertrand Russell. His analysis there of a simple table remains a stark confrontation of the view that physical reality is readily understandable.15


However, the deepest and most haunting challenge to reality, for me, comes from Immanuel Kant. He draws a firm distinction between the appearance of things and things in themselves.16 Objects as they appear to us, that we ‘intuit’ through our senses, he calls ‘phenomena’, while objects ‘as they are’ he terms ‘noumena’.17 According to this theory, the things and objects of our everyday world, those that we confidently perceive are, rather, representations of these things and objects. In contrast, we cannot have direct knowledge of the noumenal - ‘we cannot possibly know it by the senses’.18 While the noumenal is unknowable, the phenomenal is the world of our everyday experience.19 Our ordinary conception of the ‘real world’ may be illusory because reality transcends and may bear no relation to our subjective experience. Kant’s theory, as summarized above, is usually referred to as ‘transcendental idealism’.20 It has been the subject of furious philosophical debate, but, in common with much metaphysics, it encourages us to question the relationship between appearance and reality.


Recent work in cognitive science explains some of what Kant says in less abstract terms. In The Case against Reality: How Evolution Hid the Truth from Our Eyes, Donald Hoffman explores reality from the perspective of evolutionary psychology. Drawing on countless studies and experiments, he hypothesizes that ‘if our perceptions were shaped by natural selection then they almost surely evolved to hide reality’.21 What we experience when we see, smell, touch, hear, and taste are not veridical, which means they’re not accurate representations or reflections of reality. Instead, our senses have evolved, essentially to keep us alive, so we can survive long enough to reproduce, pass along our genes, and nurture our children. Hoffman maintains we’ve evolved a sensory apparatus that is geared not to perceiving faithfully what is out there, but to ensuring survival. He concludes that ‘the probability is zero that we see reality as it is’.22 He fleshes this out in the following terms:


We see objects in three dimensions not because we reconstruct objective reality, but because this is the format of a compression algorithm that evolution happened to build into us.…We live and move and have our being not in an objective reality of spacetime and objects, but in a data structure with a format of spacetime and objects.23


The use of the term ‘data structure’ is provocative here, challenging for me the sensations I feel before and after putting on my VR headset—the qualia when squeezing a tennis ball or walking the plank.


Kant and Hoffman are making overlapping (although subtly different) points. Bryan Magee captures their common thrust when he writes that there may be ‘whole universes’ of ‘inapprehensible reality’ that are permanently unknowable, ‘unconceptualizable’ to humans.24 By this he is not just saying that some reality might be as directly inaccessible to us as the world that we see is to a blind person.25 He is suggesting there are likely to be whole realms, universes, that are permanently unknowable to humans.


Another line here would be to reflect on the spookiness of quantum physics and how, in the words of Carlo Rovelli, ‘reality is not what it seems’.26 Or we could return again to the senses of animals: ‘the platypus is a cautionary tale: it reminds us…that our experience is not the same as reality. We remain insensible to aspects of reality.’27 Animals, like us, are contained in their respective Umwelten—our own limited, sensory bubbles.


But whatever way we cut the cake, my call in this chapter is for an open-mindedness when I suggest that humans may spend much of their time in virtual reality in years to come. I am inviting a form of what has been called ‘Kantian humility’,28 a willingness to accept that our everyday conception of reality may be misleading and may be both challenged and extended by AI.


Once shaken a little into thinking that our common-sense ideas about reality may be flawed, it may not then seem so perverse at least to contemplate the idea that we might be living in a digital simulation. When I imagine that we are, I can’t help then wondering about our creators. Might they be biological intelligences or some kind of more traditional deity? Or is our layer of reality itself digitally generated? In the end, might the whole edifice of the known and unknown universe be 1s and 0s all the way down?29


Who knows? But one of the joys of AI is that it prompts deeper consideration of the human condition, of reality and our perception of reality, and, as I show in the next chapter, of the nature and future of the cosmos itself.
