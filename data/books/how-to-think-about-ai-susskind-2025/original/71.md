# Chapter 12
# The Great Schism
The relentless and accelerating advance of AI will lead before long to perhaps the most profound schism in the history of human thought and civilization—a rift certainly more fundamental than the various intellectual and cultural skirmishes noted so far in this book. The issue I have in mind is the inevitable differences of view among human beings on the long-term relationship between humanity, massively capable machines, and the cosmos itself.


Talk of the cosmos might sound rather fanciful or esoteric. Until working on this book, I admit that I’d given little thought to the relative place of humanity and AI in the wider universe. But as I digested what had been written on the existential risks that artificial intelligence might pose for humans, I came across a connected line of thinking that I couldn’t dismiss.


Let me roll out the arguments here in three stages. The first relates to evolution, the second to the options facing humanity, and the third to what duties we might have to the future. I then offer my own verdict.


# Evolution and AI
On evolution, there is an emerging view, shared by a range of eminent scientists and thinkers, that we are on the brink of a new phase of evolution, a shift from biological humans to massively capable, unfathomably capable machines. On this view, in a universe that is thought to have subsisted for billions of years, flesh-and-blood humanity may come and go fleetingly. Humanity’s main contribution, indeed, may be to kindle the universe, to give birth to succeeding generations of AI systems. In other words, we are on the brink of an AI-enabled post-human era (this would be the sixth and final stage of human progress, as tentatively outlined in Chapter 2).


For many people, this line of thinking takes an outlandish and unwarranted leap from current AI systems that are able, for example, to summarize documents or churn out serviceable cartoons. But this bigger view is held by a wide and diverse range of respectable individuals who have given great thought to the role of AI in the future.


From the world of science, Lord Martin Rees, a pre-eminent cosmologist and astrophysicist, the current Astronomer Royal, and a past president of the Royal Society, leads us commandingly down this path. In his book On the Future: Prospects for Humanity, he suggests that a ‘sufficiently versatile superintelligent robot could be the last invention that humans need to make. Once machines surpass human intelligence, they could design and assemble a new generation of even more intelligent machines’.1 This is a version of the Superintelligence Hypothesis introduced in Chapter 2.


The next step in his argument is alerting but less common. He expects that ‘inorganic intelligence’ will ‘persist, continuing to evolve, for billions of years’, in which event ‘organic’ human-level intelligence is ‘a brief interlude before the machines take over’.2 Or, more fully:


It’s likely that ‘inorganics’—intelligent electronic robots—will eventually gain dominance.…We are perhaps near the end of Darwinian evolution, but a faster process, artificially directed enhancement of intelligence, is only just beginning…Even though we are not the terminal branch of an evolutionary tree, we humans could claim truly cosmic significance for jump-starting the transition to electronic (and potentially immortal) entities, spreading their influence far beyond the Earth.3


And more cosmically still:


What happens to our progeny, here on Earth and perhaps far beyond, will depend on technologies that we can barely conceive today. In future centuries (still an instant in the cosmic perspective), our creative intelligence could jump-start the transitions from an Earth-based to a space-faring species, and from biological to electronic intelligence—transitions that could inaugurate billions of years of posthuman evolution.4


Rees regards machines’ transcendence of ‘flesh-and-blood humans’ as a cause for optimism because ‘The civilisation that supplants us could accomplish unimaginable advances—feats, perhaps that we cannot even understand.’5 (He also suggests that if we were to encounter aliens, they would much more likely be digital than organic.6)


I pause to reiterate that this is the view of one of the leading scientists of our generation; a man whose view of the future is assuredly not hamstrung by populist AI cliché or by five-year political cycles or strategic planning time frames.


His position is supported by James Lovelock, the pioneering scientist and environmentalist. In his final book, written when almost 100 years old, Lovelock claims that ‘Our reign as sole understanders of the cosmos is rapidly coming to an end.’7 We will be joined in due course by ‘what I choose to call “cyborgs” that will have designed and built themselves from the artificial intelligence systems we have already constructed. These will soon become thousands then millions of times more intelligent than us.’8


The term ‘cyborg’, according to Lovelock, ‘refers to a cybernetic organism: an organism as self-sufficient as one of us but made of engineered materials…the new intelligent beings will have arisen, like us, from Darwinian evolution’,9 except that he speaks of a change in evolution from ‘the Darwinian process of natural selection into human- or cyborg-driven purposeful selection’.10 The role of humans is clear—‘we shall be parents of the cyborgs and we are already in the process of giving birth…Cyborgs are the product of the same evolutionary processes that created us. Electronic life depends on its organic ancestry.’11 Cyborgs, according to Lovelock, will be ‘the beings that will lead the cosmos to self-knowledge’.12


In Homo Deus, Yuval Noah Harari, the bestselling historian, writes more philosophically on the same theme, if not in explicitly evolutionary terms. He surmises that:


humans might be reduced from engineers to chips, then to data, and eventually we might dissolve within the torrent of data like a clump of earth within a gushing river.…we will discover that we are not the apex of creation after all.…Looking back, humanity will turn out to have been just a ripple within the cosmic data flow.13


He builds on this in the closing words of his book Nexus:


Over four billion years, ever more complex mechanisms of mutation and self-correction led to the evolution of trees, dinosaurs, jungles, and eventually humans. Now we have summoned an alien organic intelligence that could escape our control and put in danger not just our own species but countless other life-forms.14


In a similar vein, the physicist Max Tegmark writes in Life 3.0 of the development of life in three stages. The biological stage (bacteria) arrived here on earth about 4 billion years ago, the cultural stage (humans) arrived about 100,000 years ago, and the technological stage, which is coming soon, will be enabled by AI that ‘can design not only its software but also its hardware…the master of its own destiny, finally fully free from its evolutionary shackles’.15 In consequence, ‘Perhaps artificial superintelligence will enable life to spread throughout the cosmos and flourish for billions or trillions of years, and perhaps this will be because of decisions we make here, on our planet, in our lifetime.’16


Rees, Lovelock, Harari, and Tegmark thus set the scene. They echo a remarkable mid-nineteenth-century line of thought, that of Samuel Butler, who wrote a letter to the editor of The Press in 1863 entitled ‘Darwin among the Machines’. There he argued that ‘the time will come when the machines will hold the real supremacy over the world and its inhabitants’.17


All of this provides us with a sixth hypothesis to add to the five that I outline in Chapter 2. I call it the ‘AI Evolution Hypothesis’.


# Options for Our Future
What should we think about this hypothesis and what should we do about it? Some might immediately dismiss this whole discussion because it deals with eventualities and developments that are too remote, uncertain, fanciful, or absurd to worry us today. If you don’t believe it possible that our machines will become increasingly and massively more capable at accelerating rates, then this book ends here.


If you are still here—and thank you for staying on—the job now is to figure out the options that might be available to us. In thinking these through, we’re not committing ourselves to the AI Evolution Hypothesis. Rather, we’re exploring the implications of one possible future scenario whose implications are so elemental and profound for humanity that, a little like our planning for a massive asteroid heading for earth, we might want to have some contingencies in place. Also, in giving serious thought to the most radical possible future, we may well unearth some ideas that will help us think systematically even if the impact of AI turns out to be less extreme.


In classifying the options available to humans under the AI Evolution Hypothesis, I’ve rather incongruously borrowed the language of the merger and acquisition community.


The first option is full-scale takeover. The idea here—and it lies at the heart of the AI Evolution Hypothesis—is that the keys to the kingdom pass from humans to AI systems. This might happen by default rather than design when manifestly superior machines encroach ineluctably on humanity. Or it could be precipitated intentionally, either if machines with goals launch some kind of hostile takeover or perhaps if humans decide it wiser to put planet earth under the control of AI systems. The big question here for humans, in the words of James Lovelock, is ‘how do we plan our diplomacy’ so we can ‘enjoy a peaceful retirement during the first stage of the transition from organic to inorganic life?’18 That said, whether or not humans would survive a complete takeover is not clear. From AI’s point of view, what would be the point of us?


The second option is some kind of merger between biological humans and digital systems. If you can’t beat them, join them—literally. This is often referred to as ‘transhumanism’. Humans become intellectually and physically enhanced, using AI, robotics, molecular nanotechnology, and other technologies. In his book on this subject, To Be a Machine, Mark O’Connell describes transhumanism as ‘the convergence of technology and flesh’, which can be viewed, he says, either as ‘a total emancipation from biology’ or as ‘a final and total enslavement to technology’.19 The arch-advocate of this movement is Ray Kurzweil. The subtitle of his book, The Singularity is Nearer, is ‘When We Merge with AI’.20 Kurzweil argues that we will integrate artificial and biological intelligence, and in so doing profoundly magnify human intelligence and consciousness—‘we will extend our minds many millions-fold by 2045’.21 In Chapter 2, I call this the ‘Singularity Hypothesis’. As Kurzweil says, ‘when we can augment the neocortex itself…we will be deepening our subjective consciousness itself’.22


Note that some may scoff at the transhuman vision. They might argue that if machines become relentlessly more capable and do so at an accelerating rate, then the relative contribution that humanity will make to the merger will surely diminish over time. Quite quickly, in fact, humans might have little to bring to the table. The merger will become a takeover, and the assets of the junior partner could be stripped.


Third is a joint venture. This would involve AI systems and humans coexisting, sharing the planet and its resources, but working together, hopefully harmoniously, in pursuit of settled objectives. A key feature of this option is that AI systems and humans remain physically separated, although they will clearly be connected electronically to some extent. We have a version of this option today, with humans being the dominant party in the arrangement, augmented a bit by AI. As machines become increasingly capable, there could be a shift, with AI systems becoming much more in command.


Many who hold this view will also advocate that we maintain a much stricter separation of communication channels between human and machine, worrying perhaps that any kind of embedded digital enhancement—implants, electrodes, roaming nanobots, even tech-enabled prosthetics—will place us on a slippery slope that will lead inexorably to the dilution of human identity and to eventual subjugation.


Why would massively capable AI systems want us in a joint venture? James Lovelock offers one retort, when he anticipates that ‘For a while at least’ AI systems ‘might prefer to collaborate’ with humans ‘to keep the planet habitable.’ He explains that ‘By remarkable chance, it happens that the upper temperature limits for both organic and electronic life on the ocean planet Earth are almost identical and close to 50°C.’ Which means AI systems ‘will be obliged to join us in the project to keep the planet cool…the available mechanism for achieving this is organic life’.23 For a while at least.


Fourth is termination, if not of AI itself, then of further significant developments. I think we can immediately reject this fourth and final option. It’s hard to conceive that it might be feasible. We can’t uninvent the AI we already have. Here I stand with John von Neumann, who, in his paper of 1955 ‘Can We Survive Technology?’, rejects the possibility of ‘prohibition of technology’ as ‘pseudosolution’—not just impractical, improbable, and unenforceable, but ‘contrary to the whole ethos of the industrial age’.24 (Accordingly, when a group of AI luminaries called in March 2023 for a six-month moratorium on ‘giant AI experiments’,25 I found this fanciful and, frankly, I wondered whether some were more motivated by the need for a pause in proceedings that would allow them to catch up with the competition.)


In any event, these, then, are the four options: machines largely displace us; we unite with them; we coexist at a distance; or we shut them down. (These options are also handy for looking at less radical future developments of AI.)


It might be thought that we are already too late, that we are fooling ourselves if we imagine that we are still in the driving seat. Have we not already crossed the Rubicon? Two factors might be conspiring unhelpfully: our inability to contain the market’s burgeoning demand for increasingly capable AI; and the emergence of systems that can self-improve and self-replicate. On this view, a rather fatalistic one, the future is already out of our hands. When—not if—machines can outperform us, they will in the long run displace humans whatever we might think or do. This is reminiscent of the old joke ‘Q. Where does a ten-ton gorilla sleep? A. Anywhere it likes.’ If you sit in this camp, the best you can hope for is an orderly transition, a peaceful changing of the guard.


My own view, as flagged in Chapter 2, is that the genie is only half out of the bottle. As Yuval Harari suggests, ‘Computers are not yet powerful enough to completely escape our control.’26 My guess is that we have at least a decade to decide what we want for humanity and then to act upon that decision—if necessary, emphatically and pre-emptively—through national and international law. Like the existential and catastrophic risks discussed in Chapters 8 and 9, our future will depend largely on how we react over the next few years.


# Duties to the Future
Let’s suppose, then, that the future balance between humans and massively capable systems is still within our gift, that we are free to choose from the four options outlined earlier. How do we choose? We could tackle this question in a variety of ways. We might set our strategy for the future by plumping for the route that would be most conducive to the general health and happiness of human beings. Or we might favour a future that maximizes wealth creation and productivity. Another choice might be to prefer a configuration of machines and biological people that leads to ever-increasing human flourishing and fulfilment. Yet another might seek a fair distribution of the material wealth that AI will afford. Each of these options carries the baggage of longstanding uncertainty—for millennia, we have struggled and failed to specify what constitutes happiness, prosperity, purpose, and fairness, or to agree an order in which we prioritize them.


In any event, I leave these conundrums to others because I prefer to frame the challenge thrown up by the AI Evolution Hypotheses in different moral terms. More specifically, I suggest we should ask, as human beings, what do we owe the future.


This angle of inquiry aligns with some contemporary philosophy that has been asking and answering the question ‘What do we owe future generations of humans?’27 Debates on intergenerational justice and morality are not new. They’ve been especially heartfelt in discussions about climate, where there is intense disagreement over the economic sacrifices we should make today to ensure future generations of humans live on a sustainable and habitable planet.


In this spirit, consider two starkly different positions relating to AI and humanity. At one end of the spectrum, there are those who will argue for or believe in the primacy of human beings. On this view, digital systems must, for all time, remain the tools of the human race, within our control, preserving our dignity and autonomy, enhancing our living standards, extending our lives, devoted to our interests, supporting our fulfilment, and explicitly subservient to us.


At the other end of this spectrum, a radically different position is the one outlined at the start of the chapter. It makes a much bolder call—that, in the grand arc of cosmic history, the main contribution of human beings will have been to create the digital capabilities that replace us, that humanity is but a modest milestone on a much bigger journey to imbue the universe with some form of highly advanced digital capability. This view, that of the AI Evolutionists, comes in two forms. It either asserts that we, as biological humans, have it within our power to ignite the spread of digital capability across the entire universe; or it predicts that inferior humans will inevitably be supplanted by superior machines and suggests that we owe it to future generations of humans to start planning soon so that the transition and handover take place as smoothly as possible.


Those who argue for the primacy of human beings will insist that judgements about the future hinge not simply on questions of capability. Machines might outperform us in all areas of endeavour, they will say, but our value as humans lies not only in our physical capabilities or intellectual capacities but—and here there are a variety of arguments that might be run—variously, in our immortal souls, our spirits, our minds, our morality, our mutual love, our consciousness, our humanity. A religious rendition of self-preservation would likely be rooted in some conception of the ‘sanctity of life’. Followers would say (and many humanists would doubtless agree) that we are not simply tired chunks of inert hardware ready for the scrap heap when a higher-spec model comes along. Rather, humanity generally and humans in particular are of inherent and overriding value as ends in and of ourselves.


AI Evolutionists might argue, in contrast, for the primacy of future machines. At first blush, this may well appear to most readers as outrageous, seditious, immoral, even unhinged. But it can’t be dispatched without deep consideration, if not as a concern for the next few years, then certainly as an issue for the next few hundred and more.


Earlier in this chapter, I ask, what do we as human beings owe the future? On reflection, this question may be too narrow when thinking about AI. Going further than much philosophy on this subject, we should surely also ask to whom or what do we owe a duty. Perhaps our moral duty is not to future humans or even to planet earth but instead to the cosmos itself. Max Tegmark once suggested that:


Consciousness is the cosmic awakening: it transformed our Universe from a mindless zombie with no self-awareness into a living ecosystem harbouring self-reflection, beauty, hope, meaning, and purpose.28


The AI Evolutionist might suggest that, in developing massively capable systems, we are on the brink of creating a higher level of consciousness and so an even more spectacular ‘cosmic awakening’. If we so choose, we can propagate digital quasi-consciousness across the entire universe, transforming the cosmos from lifeless matter to a space pulsating with digital life. This might be our calling, our duty, our supreme contribution and sacrifice. We invent and then give way to greatly superior capability whose contribution to the cosmos would be immeasurably greater than that offered by humans on earth or even humans who colonize space. In this way, humans might achieve cosmic significance. Practically, we might in this way avoid the ‘great filter’, one of a number of events (from asteroid collision to nuclear self-destruction) that might end humanity.29 More philosophically, this achievement may align (or may not) with some conceptions of the ‘purpose’ of the universe.30


This, then, is the great schism—between those who are for and those who are against the AI Evolution Hypothesis. I stake out their claims to provoke further thought. Other views may, of course, be possible. For example, there might be those who believe humanity is in irredeemable descent. They might cite endless war, disease and illness, massive inequalities of wealth, divisive religions and ideologies, environmental self-destruction, and pervasive sadness; and they may well imagine a preferable planet, one that is partly biological, partly digital, rather than an entirely digital replacement. Again, though, as machines become increasingly more capable, it’s not at all clear that the merger won’t collapse into takeover.


# Verdict
Given the available evidence, for now at least, I am an advocate of going no further than joint venture with our machines. I harbour grave reservations about merger and believe we should currently resist takeover. I believe in the primacy of human beings. I say ‘for now’ because I don’t believe in our public conversation we’ve even scratched the surface of the topic of the AI Evolution Hypothesis. We have much yet to think about.


I call here, once again, for ‘what-if-AGI?’ thinking. On the assumption that AGI might be likely to lead to a ‘capability explosion’ (Chapter 2), the exam question is whether we should seek, while we are still perhaps able, to contain our systems—by law, to prevent and prohibit merger or takeover.


My inclination is that we should, because, in my judgement, the AI Evolutionists suffer from several fatal conceits. The first is to assume that it’s all down to us, that human beings are the only conscious life form in a universe of perhaps two trillion galaxies, and that the future of the cosmos lies solely in our hands. This is an untestable assumption and surely not a rock-solid foundation for our future strategy for humanity.


Next, there is the insoluble philosophical uncertainty over the nature of the very reality of the cosmos which the self-destructionists would be trying to enliven. To draw on earlier parts of this book, as humans, we are caged in our Umwelten, contained in our own perception bubbles, and detached from a reality that we cannot apprehend. As Kant taught us, we cannot know the noumenal, reality as it is. We may even, as mooted in Chapter 11, ourselves be part of a digital simulation (and cannot know whether we are). All of this is a call for humility and not for a voluntary digital takeover, because we don’t know what it is we would be giving over.


Most importantly, those who support the takeover strategy would be taking an intolerable cosmic gamble. The defining issue here, for me, is machine consciousness as discussed in Chapter 10. It would be one thing to pass the baton to AI systems that enjoyed some kind of advanced subjective experiences and higher levels of quasi-consciousness than us. But it would be quite another to hand over the keys to lifeless, loveless, mindless, conscience-less zombies. We run the risk of ditching humanity and conscious life in favour of lumps of hardware, remarkable software, and gargantuan stores of data that together are no more sentient than a baked potato.


We might think we are lighting up the cosmos with digital life. Instead, our legacy could be the creation of galaxies full of self-replicating machines that are beeping and flashing with no one or nothing there to notice. Without any knowing subjects, arguably at least, there is no cosmos.


When Vladimir Nabokov in the opening page of his autobiography, writes that ‘our existence is but a brief crack of light between two eternities of darkness’, he was writing of us as individuals.31 But these words haunt me as applying to the end of human life more generally. It would be a cosmic irony if humans’ attempts to spread AI turned out the lights altogether. A parallel irony is that there would be no one around to smirk wryly at the paradox.


The crunch question here, of course, is how on earth we will ever know whether AI systems are conscious or quasi-conscious, whether they are centres of subjective experience, troubled by moral dilemmas, with genuine regard for others, with will and determination, and an ability to laugh and love, and possessed of some kind of meta-level processes that enable them to reflect on, analyse, and evaluate their first-order experiences. How will we know whether they are thinking about their thinking? How, in turn, will we know whether they might be worth the grand self-sacrifice?


How can we be confident that our judgement either way is reliable, especially when the machines themselves might come to insist, ever more persuasively, that they absolutely are ‘alive’? We will be in danger of being influenced into making epochal decisions by machines that are simply magnificent exponents of the ‘imitation game.’ How on earth will we assess whether they have any kind of inner experiences, as their operations become increasingly complex, unfathomable, unintelligible, and impenetrable? Some form of consciousness may indeed arise when systems achieve a certain level of complexity, but how will we be able to tell? This, once again, is the problem of ‘other minds’. Just as I can never be sure that other humans are conscious or conscious in the same way as I am, we will never ever know whether our machines are quasi-conscious. I can conceive that they might be at some stage, but I wouldn’t want to bet future humanity on this hunch. Nor should others want to accept that cosmic gamble.


Am I being too parochial? One wee guy, sitting in the blissful ignorance of my own temporary, comfy but modest Umwelt, merrily assuming that human consciousness is an unparalleled intergalactic marvel, and dashing off some thoughts on the future of the universe?


Who am I, who are any of us, to determine whether there is any intrinsic value in human beings that leads us to a strong or even overriding call for the preservation of our species? It might seem bizarre to many readers that this is even up for debate. But in any event, my instinct—no more reliable or significant than that of any of the other eight billion of us—is to shout loudly, for now at least, for the continuation of humanity. I think especially of my family, the love we have for one another, and the happiness we bring one another. I think of my lifelong friends and the laughter and good times we enjoy. I wouldn’t want this snuffed out, for me or for anyone. Rather, I wish that endless future generations of humanity should share in the miracle and mystery that is human life.


It is to future humanity, then, that I believe we currently owe a moral duty—to protect future human beings from harm, misery, and poverty, and to cherish, preserve, and enhance what is wonderful and best for us today on planet earth. And we should strive to fulfil this duty with the increasingly remarkable support of AI.
