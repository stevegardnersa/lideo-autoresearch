# Conclusion
If we are to think responsibly about the impact of AI on humanity, we must look well beyond today’s technologies. In so doing, we need to understand the context of AI—its history, its trajectory, and the factors that are driving its advance. And we should focus as much on the outcomes of these remarkable systems as on how they work. It would be invaluable also to equip ourselves with a new vocabulary to help us conceptualize and discuss more coherently the seismic shifts that AI will bring to all aspects of our lives.


AI’s reach will extend well beyond the automation of current human activity. It will transform how we live and work, and lead to radical changes to the long-standing institutions and structures that underpin our current society. We are still at an early stage in assessing the ramifications of this transformation.


Profound risks come hand in hand with the great potential benefits of AI. To give ourselves the best chance of balancing the advantages and threats of future systems, we must call up an interdisciplinary army of our best and brightest humans to help harness artificial intelligence.


We should think philosophically too. We need to work through the consequences of coexisting with AI systems that might appear to enjoy consciousness. We should reflect deeply on AI that might communicate directly with human brains and on systems that can create extraordinary virtual worlds. And we should allow ourselves to contemplate the relevance of increasingly capable systems for the evolution of biological humans.


Our job in thinking clearly about AI is ceaselessly challenged by the pace at which technology is advancing. Our systems are becoming more capable at an accelerating rate. In planning for the long term, we should be more interested in GPT-7 (as a concept) than the limitations of GPT-4.


What is perhaps toughest to grasp is that not yet invented technologies, some of which are currently inconceivable, will have far greater impact on us in the 2030s than the tools we have today.


To help us cope with all this uncertainty, to stretch our thinking, and to lay some foundations in this quicksand, I call loudly for what-if-AGI? thinking—in government, business, education, and beyond. In this proposed thought experiment, I ask that we assume future AI systems will rival or outperform humans in almost all cognitive tasks. Our job, then, is to assess the impact and implications of such systems. Technologically, would this lead inescapably to massively capable systems that are superintelligent? What ethical constraints would we want to impose on these systems? What would this mean for our jobs? How would we share the wealth they would create? How would we regulate them? Even if AGI doesn’t come to pass, this thinking will help us in our management of more modest AI.


We are shying away from these difficult questions when we fixate on generative AI, for this is simply the latest AI and certainly not the last. Failing to look squarely at AGI as a probable future would be unforgiveable. It would expose us to a catalogue of foreseeable threats. More, it will discourage us from deploying AI positively and in its pivotal role—that of meeting humanity’s greatest challenges.
