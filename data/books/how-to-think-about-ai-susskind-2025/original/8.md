# Personal Note
I’ve been thinking about AI since 1981, when I stumbled into the field as a law student at the University of Glasgow. I was writing a dissertation on whether computers might replace judges, and all avenues in my research led me to artificial intelligence. I quickly became hooked and keen to delve deeper. So I left my native Scotland for the University of Oxford and wrote my doctorate there on AI and the law in the mid 80s.1 Later that decade, while running the UK artificial intelligence group of a global accountancy and consulting firm, I co-developed the world’s first fully operational AI system for lawyers.2 I have since written several AI-related books, not just on law but also, with Daniel Susskind, on the impact of AI across the professions, including health, education, tax, audit, architecture, journalism, and more.3 Today, much of my time is devoted to advising international professional firms and national governments on what AI means for their future.


All in all, then, artificial intelligence has been a lifelong preoccupation and fascination. This book is an introductory guide to the subject, and it frequently draws on my own travels in the field. It also borrows from my background in four disciplines that converge in AI—philosophy, digital technology, law, and long-term strategic planning—and from my work over the years across the private, public, voluntary, and educational sectors.


In what appears to many as an outlandish family business, my two sons also spend much of their time thinking and writing about AI—Daniel, as an economist, exploring the impact of technology on employment and on economic growth,4 and Jamie, as a lawyer and political theorist, looking at the regulation of AI and at its impact on politics.5 This book includes much of their thinking, which is reflected in my shamelessly frequent citations of their work.


While preparing this book and in thinking intensely about the latest developments in AI, my own perspective shifted. I have for long been optimistic that AI can bring great social and economic benefits. I’m now even more enthused. But as I’ve immersed myself in recent and staggering advances, I’ve also become more sharply aware of the range and scale of the threats that AI poses.


I now believe that balancing the benefits and threats of artificial intelligence—saving humanity with and from AI—is the defining challenge of our age.
