# Introduction
Marvin Minsky was a professor of computer science at MIT. He was one of the early AI pioneers and the winner in 1969 of the prestigious Turing Award. The following year, in LIFE magazine, he was quoted as saying that ‘Once the computers get control, we might never get it back. We would survive at their sufferance. If we’re lucky, they might decide to keep us as pets.’1


I used to think this was funny. I’m not sure now.


A different view was set out in the New Scientist in 1986: ‘AI’s record of barefaced public deception is unparalleled in the annals of academic study.’2 In the decades since, many scholars and observers have echoed this sweeping indictment, claiming that AI is largely hype.


How do we make sense of the gulf between Minsky and the New Scientist?


In truth, AI has always divided human beings. Since the mid-1950s, when the term ‘artificial intelligence’ was first coined, fans and critics have been equally vocal. The tension between the proponents and naysayers extends well beyond the usual conflicts that simmer between thinkers and scientists of different schools. There is incalculably more at stake here because—to put it starkly—AI will heavily shape the future for humans and society.


But does AI represent the apotheosis or the antithesis of humanity and civilization? Will artificial intelligence provide the answers to mankind’s gravest challenges, from climate change to global health? Or does it signal the descent of biological man?


I don’t have pat answers to these or many other pressing questions that AI is now prompting. We’re all still feeling our way through some agonizingly difficult and unmapped terrain. I’m conscious also that in the coming years there will be questions relating to AI that we are not able to conceive today—questions we haven’t yet imagined relating to problems that haven’t yet arisen from systems that haven’t yet been invented. Today we can’t see beyond the beam of the headlights that is currently cast by our early understanding of current and emerging technologies.


My hope in this maelstrom of uncertainty is to provide readers with various ways of thinking about AI. These should encourage more informed reflection and debate. This is not a technical book. It is introductory in tone and content. I assume that readers have no technical knowledge of AI and digital systems. One of my aims indeed is to demystify artificial intelligence and public discussion about this remarkable concept and to offer some assurance that you don’t need to be a technology expert to think and talk sensibly about the impact of AI. I expect that many scientists will wince when I skip across issues of great technological interest and complexity. I don’t do so lightly. However, I urge that debate about AI and its ramifications shouldn’t always be focused on the technological detail.


I was spurred to write in response to the frenzy that has followed the launch in late November 2022 of an extraordinary AI system known as ChatGPT, which belongs to a branch of artificial intelligence that is widely called ‘generative AI’ (don’t worry for now about what this means). ChatGPT attracted 100 million users in two months and almost double that figure within one year of launch. An avalanche of commentary and speculation has followed. Yet, as I show, many current claims and popular predictions about AI are muddled. There is, for example, a sense from many who are new to the field that generative AI represents the end game, the final destination in the world of artificial intelligence. In truth, we are simply warming up. It is early days for AI.


I am concerned that debate and policymaking on AI is being too heavily influenced by technologists and tech entrepreneurs. While the prodigious talent of the best of them cannot be denied, some pronounce too dogmatically on issues like ethics, social impact, international relations, and regulation. Moreover, they are often too vested in how and why they themselves do AI to speak reliably or objectively about its future and effects.


My aim at this remarkable juncture in human history is to bring greater clarity to our everyday conversation about AI, which explains why my focus is on how we, as humans, might usefully think about AI.


The book is organized in five parts, each corresponding to what I take to be the basic building blocks in thinking clearly about AI.


Part One is devoted to understanding what AI is all about. Chapter 1 suggests and shows that a good way to climb the AI learning curve is to look at its history and trajectory over the past seventy-five years. To put some flesh on the bones, Chapter 2 zooms out and encourages AI to be viewed in the broader context of more general developments in digital technology.


Part Two invites readers to think differently. Chapter 3 identifies two broad but deeply contrasting ways of looking at AI, one focusing on the outcomes of AI systems, the other preoccupied, in several ways, with the underlying processes. Chapter 4 unravels various confusions that flow from ignoring the differences between these two perspectives, not least over the question of whether humanity is doomed because of AI. Chapter 5 turns to machine capabilities for which we don’t currently have a vocabulary and along the way explores some apparent limitations of AI systems.


Part Three is more practical, focusing on making AI work. Chapter 6 argues that to grasp the impact of AI on labour, we need to think well beyond the automation and substitution of current human activity; rather, we must grasp the way that AI can transform how we work and sometimes even eliminate the problems to which many human jobs are currently devoted. Chapter 7 warns against simply grafting AI onto existing organizations and calls instead for radical structural change to our institutions, including our health services, justice systems, schools, and universities.


Looking to the downside of AI, Part Four confronts the risks arising from AI. Chapter 8 categorizes the wide range of relevant threats, and Chapter 9 offers some thoughts on tackling them.


Part Five contemplates the future for humanity when our machines become much more capable. It is more philosophical, speculative, and, I expect, unnerving. Chapter 10 discusses whether machines can be conscious. Chapter 11 urges that we shouldn’t look exclusively at AI when we’re thinking about the future. It points to other vital technologies and, in particular, to brain/computer interfaces and virtual reality. This leads to consideration of some disconcerting philosophical possibilities relating to the nature of everyday reality. Finally, and again big-picture, Chapter 12 suggests we should think of AI in evolutionary and cosmological terms when contemplating its impact on the future of humanity.


Each of the chapters confronts issues of great complexity. My hope is not to dispose of the questions asked but to help readers to begin their own discussions—with family, friends, colleagues, and their governments—about the impact of the most consequential set of technologies of our time.
