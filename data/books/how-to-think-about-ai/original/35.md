# Chapter 5
# We Don’t Have the Words
Several confusions in popular thinking about AI are identified in Chapters 3 and 4. One is to fail to distinguish between process-thinking and outcome-thinking. Another is to lapse into not-us thinking, often thereby falling foul of the AI Fallacy, which is thinking that AI systems have to copy the way humans work to achieve high-level performance. Yet another is to want or expect AI systems to explain themselves in much the same fashion as humans do.


Underpinning each of these and other muddles I discuss is a more elemental problem—we do not have the vocabulary and concepts to capture and discuss the way that our increasingly capable systems work. Instead, we root our debate in language that relates to humans. In so doing, we anthropomorphize, we use metaphor, and we squeeze new ideas into old words. We see this most starkly in debates about what computers cannot do. For example, it is commonly claimed, as noted in Chapter 4, that machines cannot exercise judgement or be empathetic or creative. However, saying that machines do not function in the same way as humans may offer insight into the different processes that underpin the operations of each, but it tells us little about their capabilities. It is hard not to want to use human labels to describe what’s going on when machines deliver outputs that historically we thought were the exclusive product of biological people. We can call it metaphor, but, to stress again, speaking of machines in terms of human attributes brings confusion.


For a while, by way of further illustration, many technology companies and management consultants liked to talk about AI by referring to ‘cognitive computing’. It might have felt cutting-edge to use this term, but it was plainly wrong-headed: the systems under this heading had no more cognitive states than a grilled kipper. It was also misleading—hype, essentially—because ‘cognitive computing’ suggested capabilities that AI systems did not have.


The same goes for ‘hallucinations’, a term that is widely used to refer to the errors and fabrications to which generative AI systems are prone. At best, this is another metaphor, and at worst the word suggests cognitive states that are quite absent. Hallucinations are mistaken perceptions of sensory experiences. This really isn’t what’s going on when ChatGPT churns out gobbledygook.


Even the foundational concept in the whole field of AI—intelligence—suffers from loose usage. Long before the advent of AI, this term carried different meanings in different disciplines (such as philosophy, psychology, and cognitive science). And within these disciplines, the nature of intelligence was hotly contested—in psychology, for example, controversy raged over what it meant to say that one person was more intelligent than another. People understandably got very exercised about the very idea of ‘IQ’.


In artificial intelligence, according to Stuart Russell in his Reith Lectures on AI, ‘From the very beginnings…intelligence in machines has been defined in the same way: “Machines are intelligent to the extent that their actions can be expected to achieve their objectives.”’1 This speaks of a capacity of machines that is analogous to that of humans. In the same process-oriented vein, the pioneering AI company, DeepMind, at one stage used to stress that their mission was to ‘solve intelligence’, which puzzled me, because this of itself said little about what their systems would actually do.


Contrast this use of ‘intelligence’ with the outcome-thinking that was rife until the advent of ChatGPT, when it was commonly said that, for the foreseeable future, AI would be ‘narrow’. This meant it would be able only to exhibit human-level performance in restricted fields. It would be many years yet, if ever, the argument continued, before AI would be AGI—that is, artificial general intelligence. When reference is made to narrow AI, this is clearly about outcomes. In law, for example, narrow AI refers to a system that can operate in a single, often quite small, relatively well-bounded branch of the legal system rather than a machine that can solve any and all legal problems.


And so, even the basic concept of ‘intelligence’ is used confusingly in AI—sometimes to refer to how humans or systems function, and sometimes to what they can do. Here, as elsewhere in AI, we need a richer vocabulary. We do not have the words.


# Language
As we move from one stage of human and social development to another, words and concepts often acquire new meanings or are concocted to capture and reflect emerging ideas for which no pre-existing vocabulary is adequate. Eric Hobsbawm illustrates this point memorably on the opening page of his book, The Age of Revolution: 1789–1848. He invites us there to ‘consider a few English words which were invented, or gained their modern meanings’ during the sixty-year period that was the object of his study:


They are words such as ‘industry’, ‘industrialist’, ‘factory’, ‘middle class’, ‘working class’, ‘capitalism’ and ‘socialism’. They include ‘aristocracy’ as well as ‘railway’, ‘liberal’ and ‘conservative’ as political terms, ‘nationality’, ‘scientist’ and ‘engineer’…‘statistics’, ‘sociology’ and several other names of modern sciences, ‘journalism’ and ‘ideology’, are all coinages or adaptations of this period.2


Isn’t it remarkable that these terms were not in play before the Industrial Revolution? What new words or repurposed words, I wonder, will emerge in the coming years to refer to the new concepts, ideas, and problems that have been birthed in the age of AI?


My task now is to respond to this question partially, by discussing three concepts for which we need new words. They relate to judgement, empathy, and creativity, phenomena that are arguably not exhibited by AI systems. In each case, I propose that we should not use the term that we are comfortable in applying to humans. Instead I append the prefix ‘quasi-’, Latin for ‘as if’.3


I imply no ranking here. I’m not saying that systems that exhibit quasi-judgement, quasi-empathy, and quasi-creativity perform less well than human judgement, empathy, and creativity. I am simply finding a way to distinguish between human and machine processes and outcomes that are superficially similar. I apologize for not having the guts or imagination to come up with new terms, but my historic attempts at neologizing have not ended well.


# Judgement and Quasi-Judgement
Let’s start with judgement. In law, lawyers often challenge my thinking about AI, claiming something like, ‘a machine is incapable of exercising judgement’ (and, by extension, of course, that their jobs are safe because they are all about judgement). Doctors say something similar, referring to ‘clinical judgement’ as apparently the essence of fine healthcare and, it so happens, clearly beyond the reach of AI. Accountants too stress that ‘audit judgement’, which so far has been human judgement, cannot be replicated by machine—no machine, I am told, can ‘smell a rat’, an achievement only feasible when visiting a business in person and ‘kicking the tyres’. Once again, of course, this is all process-thinking, confusing judgement (the facility that human professionals deploy) with outcome (what the community wants—health, being kept out of trouble, assurance about the market). It’s also not-us thinking, the defensive gambit, the special pleading, of those who are inclined to close ranks around their reputations and ways of working.


The judgement I have in mind here is of the variety that professionals bring to bear in their work. There are other types of judgement—for example, aesthetic judgement and moral judgement—which are not my current concern. In the context of professional work, we can reasonably be asked to confront the simmering question here—how indeed can a machine exercise judgement? But I think this is the wrong question. Or, at least, it’s an unhelpful process-based question and commits the AI Fallacy. It’s a bit like asking—how can a machine do exactly what humans do? The answer is that it cannot, but that does not dispose of the interesting issues here. A better question is a prior question—to what problem is professional judgement the answer? Or why is it that humans are called upon to exercise their judgement in the first place? People who seek expert help do not generally approach their professional advisers saying, ‘Good morning, I would like some judgement please.’ Judgement isn’t the end in itself. Rather, and in very broad terms, judgement is a tool or a facility that biological humans bring to bear when they are invited or required to handle uncertainty. Judgement is the process. Removal of uncertainty is the outcome.


Rather than asking whether machines can exercise judgement, a much better question, therefore, is—can machines handle uncertainty? As a generality, on the strength of the huge bodies of data that they embody, the answer to this is a resounding ‘yes’.


My late father was a dermatologist. He was hugely supportive of my work, and we spoke a lot about AI, especially in the 1980s. I had become interested in AI and healthcare because some of the early success stories in AI were medical applications. I had the good fortune to work with some pioneers in medical AI, work that was consolidated in the research Daniel Susskind and I undertook into AI in the professions, where healthcare was one of our main objects of inquiry.


In the late 1980s, Dad and I gave some thought to building an AI system that could diagnose malignant melanoma, the most serious of skin cancers. Our first plan, very much in the spirit of the mainstream rule-based expert systems work of the time, was to build, essentially, a huge flow chart that could guide the uninitiated through the rules that skin specialists follow when assessing whether or not a lesion is nasty. This proved difficult. We found it hard to identify all the rules, and it was harder still to determine the relative weightings of those we did isolate and how they interacted.


Our second and final attempt, in 1989, was to use an early machine learning tool which deployed an algorithm (ID3, since you asked) to generate decision trees from data sets. The idea was that, rather than handcrafting the decision tree through ‘knowledge elicitation’ (basically, my trying to mine the jewels from my father’s head), we could gather data about symptoms and the results of the histology, and the system would generate the decision tree automatically. In the event, we did not have access to sufficient data, and we put the project to one side. (In passing, I find it interesting and slightly embarrassing that we thought the main use of machine learning would be to generate decision trees—but that was the way we thought about AI.)


Many years later, in 2017, Dad sent me a link to a paper published in Nature entitled ‘Dermatologist-Level Classification of Skin Cancer with Deep Neural Networks’.4 It seemed like a breakthrough. When we chatted about it, he said he knew immediately that this system would be better than him and his colleagues. He suggested that what doctors enigmatically call their instinct or gut reaction or judgement is effectively a process by which they compare a patient before them and, consciously or unconsciously, match their symptoms with their own mini-database of experiences. He said that he’d only seen a few hundred melanomas in his many years as a dermatologist. He therefore felt confident that an AI system’s database of 129,450 worrying lesions was likely to enable it to outperform him and other skin specialists, even if the underlying processes were quite unlike the working practices of seasoned physicians. An analogous argument about how human experts work could cogently be made across most professional work. The pattern-matching with extensive past experience can be formally explained as a form of ‘holistic similarity recognition’.5


# Empathy and Quasi-Empathy
Another criticism often levelled at AI systems is that they cannot be empathetic. How could a system discern the emotional state of human beings and, more importantly, how could it put itself in their shoes and share their pain and joy?


In thinking about this in early 2023, I decided to set generative AI a task. I asked the original version of ChatGPT whether it could ‘draft an empathetic email, from me, for a friend whose father died recently’. It responded as follows:


I am so sorry to hear about the loss of your father. Losing a parent is one of the most difficult things a person can go through, and my heart goes out to you and your family during this difficult time. I know that no words can truly express the depth of your grief, but please know that I am here for you and am here to support you in any way that I can. Whether you need someone to talk to, a shoulder to cry on, or just some time to yourself, please don’t hesitate to reach out to me. I am sending you all my love and condolences during this heartbreaking time.


Not perfect—there are a couple of grammatical slips—but not at all bad. It was certainly as well constructed as many of the notes of condolence that I received from my friends when my dad passed away. In fact, I was thinking about him when I read the message above, and I had tears in my eyes. I reflected at the time that we don’t have a word for what was happening to me—a concept that captures a machine making a human cry.


The experiment leads me to suggest that we should exercise caution in making sweeping assumptions about AI and empathy. There are two dimensions to empathy—cognitive and affective.6 The cognitive component refers to the mental processes by which the emotional state of another is perceived and categorized. Can AI systems recognize (or quasi-recognize) emotional states? Advances in the field of affective computing suggest they can (affective computing concerns itself with systems that can both detect and express emotions).7 Related research in this field gives us reasons to expect that AI systems will in due course be better than biological people in identifying the disposition of humans. More, it is entirely predictable that AI systems before long will be able to respond to users in a way that will appear to be more empathetic than most humans—a combination of generative AI, emotional and psychological profiling, and speech synthesis technology (providing a compassionate voice, maybe). I refer readers here to the interesting work of Jeremy Howick, who has been working on the idea of an ‘Empathy Turing Test’—‘can a human user distinguish between the empathy showed by an artificial carer and that showed by a human practitioner?’8


Just because machines might make the right noises, however, this doesn’t mean that they are somehow looking at the world through their user’s eyes or feeling and sharing their pain and joy. This would call for some kind of replication of the second component of empathizing, the affective component, and would assume that AI systems could enjoy some form of consciousness (an unwarranted leap, as I discuss in Chapter 10).


Here, therefore, we may have found the boundaries of machine empathy—AI systems, it seems, cannot put themselves in the shoes of humans and vicariously experience their emotions. One response here is to point out that we can never really know whether humans who are apparently being empathetic are, in fact, anywhere near our shoes. They could be dissembling, pretending, or performing, consciously or otherwise. Truth to tell, we cannot know for sure whether any person has any feelings for us. It seems most likely that they do, but we cannot look directly into what philosophers call ‘other minds’.


Given advances in AI, however, it is probable that in due course AI systems will be better at simulating empathy (faking it) than insincere humans and may even be able to stimulate a preferable emotional reaction in their users than sincere humans.


All of which leads me to say, until we have a better word for the job, it makes sense and is helpful to say that AI systems can indeed be quasi-empathetic.


# Creativity and Quasi-Creativity
On creativity and AI, the main issues here were ventilated as early as 1984 by Donald Michie (the father of AI in the UK) and Rory Johnston in their prescient book The Creative Computer. There they asked, ‘Could machines themselves conceive solutions that have eluded human minds?’ and, as an example, ‘Is it possible for computers to produce new works of art?’9


Fast-forward to one of the questions I am most commonly asked when I give talks about our book The Future of the Professions, namely ‘What jobs will there be for our children?’ Today’s senior professionals are less concerned about their own future but can see in years to come that many traditional jobs will be transformed if not replaced by increasingly capable systems. Until recently, in response to their concerns, we have often pointed to various creative industries, suggesting that these might be the last to fall. It had seemed to us, as to most commentators, that the fruits of creative human labour would not be matched by AI systems in the short to medium term at least. But this proved to be a mistake, because the latest wave of AI systems is already capable of generating poetry, prose, lyrics, music, cartoons, line drawings, landscapes, videos, and more, often now at a higher standard than most humans. Creative artists, for example, must be deeply disconcerted by the ease with which the non-creative can produce superb images for the purposes of, say, a well-illustrated deck of slides.


Despite the quality of the end product, our instincts might still be to deny that the generative AI systems that are turning out serviceable art and literature can meaningfully be said to be creative. I share this instinct, but we should be clear that this is to engage in process-thinking. This is not necessarily wrong, but it is just one perspective. It’s to focus on the human way of creating art or literature rather than the output alone. Despite the novelty of the output, it seems to matter to us if it doesn’t flow from humans’ creative juices. And we might think this even if the AI output is in some sense superior. I think it better to refer to that underlying computational process as ‘quasi-creative’, pending the formulation of a new term.


There is an underlying issue, however, that goes deeper than the label we choose to describe machines that generate novel content. The bigger question is how we as humans should regard this original content from AI systems. Is there not something fundamental missing when we look upon a sketch or when we read a short story generated by an AI system, even if it is superb?


In tackling this conundrum, consider an analogy from sport. It’s conceivable before long that there will, at some point, be robots that can run 100 metres faster than Usain Bolt or shoot lower scores on the golf course than Tiger Woods, even at their best. But would we be interested in this? You might well be if you are fascinated by robotic performance. But most of us were thrilled by Bolt and Woods in their prime precisely because they were flesh-and-blood humans like us. We revel in the achievements of sporting greats; we derive some kind of vicarious pleasure from what they do, in part because they are indeed—very, very loosely—like us. If we are runners or golfers ourselves, we know just how extraordinary these humans are. We aspire to being a bit like them. We take pleasure in their prowess. We marvel at the effort and sacrifice made in their training. It’s not just about speed and accuracy. It’s the inspiration we derive from fellow humans at their peak. It’s not just about outcomes. The way in which these outcomes are achieved is a vital and irreplaceable part of being a spectator.


So too in the arts. When we read great literature or listen to fine music or view superb paintings, part of the thrill is precisely that another human has been involved in the work—striving, communicating, creating, and, in turn, inspiring, stimulating, and elevating our lives. Again, an indispensable and intrinsic part of that experience is the knowledge that another human is at the other end. We sense, for example, when the art has been fuelled by the pain or frustration or ecstasy or exhilaration of the creator.


And so, no matter how capable our systems are, it’s likely that many forms of human expression, not least live performance, will continue to be valued by humans for their own sake. This is surely at the heart of our instinct that there is a significant difference between the creativity of humans and the quasi-creativity of machines. It’s not to deny the increasingly remarkable output of AI systems, but it is to stick by our sense that there is value in human creativity beyond the artefact itself.


That said, I accept that, now and in the future, we might not always know whether a human or a machine was responsible when we look at a drawing or read a poem or listen to a tune. In which case, we might be uncertain whether to enjoy this as the creation of a fellow biological person or the quasi-creation of an AI system. This is not entirely unchartered territory because we have for long been fooled by counterfeits and imitations. But I expect this issue will become ever more important to us and we will routinely seek some kind of authentication of tomorrow’s creations, some confirmation of their provenance, reassurance that the originator was human. Trusted intermediaries will help here. Early work on watermarking of AI-generated documents is a sign that this issue is being taken seriously.


It’s also possible that future generations of humans might think differently. If AI systems come to produce output that is incontestably and markedly superior to any human creation, I can imagine that, for some forms of quasi-creations—movies, cartoons, games—their origins may not matter.
