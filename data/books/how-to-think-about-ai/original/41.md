# Chapter 6
# Automation, Innovation, Elimination
Moving now to more practical matters but staying for a while with the brain, I was invited in 2017 to address two thousand neurosurgeons at a conference in Boston. A scary audience. The organizers had invited me to speak about the research behind our book The Future of the Professions and to answer the question ‘What is the future of neurosurgeons?’ They had encouraged me to be controversial in discussing the impact of AI and robotics on their world.


I took a deep plunge early on, proclaiming that ‘Patients don’t want neurosurgeons.’ There was a ripple of concerned murmurs in the auditorium. The conference chairman looked pallid. I moved swiftly to the punchline and said that ‘Patients want health.’ I went on to say that, for a particular kind of health problem, human neurosurgeons are unquestionably the best answer we now have, and I thanked them for their service. But I speculated that, in decades to come, people would surely look back and express disbelief that their predecessors had so often cut heads and bodies open—how primitive.


I moved then to my big point. The future of healthcare in this context, I put it to the audience, would not simply be better or even robotic surgery but would surely be non-invasive and minimally invasive therapy as well as preventative medicine.


I’ve told this story over the years as a case study in what I call ‘outcome-thinking’. It challenges professionals, for example, to focus on the results that are sought rather than our current ways of achieving these results. It stresses that the value of professional service lies largely, if not exclusively, in the outcomes delivered rather than in the techniques and methods used. More recently, however, I have seen there is another, equally significant lesson to be taken from the neurosurgeon anecdote, and this relates to a distinction I now propose—between ‘automation’, ‘innovation’, and ‘elimination’.


# Automation
By ‘automation’, I mean the computerization of what we already do. It’s the application of technology to pre-existing tasks, processes, or activities, often to impose greater efficiency. We automate or systematize, in other words, when we streamline, optimize, and even turbocharge our current practices. Automation in this sense is the central player in the story of computers so far. For eighty-five years, we’ve grafted technology onto our old ways of doing things. Automation is what most people think of when they think of digital technology.


I have little doubt that automation, as an approach to the deployment of technology, has delivered all manner of cost reductions, time savings, process improvements, productivity gains, and related benefits. More, because automation supports and enhances the status quo, this explains why many white-collar workers and professionals find it reassuringly familiar. Indeed, automation perpetuates many dimensions of traditional working methods, including much that is defective, which is why cynics sometimes say that automation gives us ‘mess for less’.


The term and the concept of automation dominate discussion about digital technology. To revert to the neurosurgery example, robotic surgery is clearly an example of automation—some or all surgical processes and activity are conducted by machine rather than human. This is also an illustration of what some economists and business consultants call ‘task substitution’—for some tasks, humans are taken out of the loop, and there is a swap, with the machine discharging the task instead of the biological person.


# Innovation
To focus only on task substitution, however, is to think that the future of surgery lies exclusively in robotic surgery—automating and replacing human surgical work. But my neurosurgery story is also—and more importantly—about ‘innovation’. I use this term in a very specific way to refer to the use of techniques and technology that enable us to do things that previously weren’t possible or even conceivable. Non-invasive or minimally invasive procedures are case studies in innovation. They are designed to secure the results or outcomes that patients want but with less pain and anxiety, fewer risks, and avoiding the often lengthy recovery times that come with conventional surgery. Non-invasive and minimally invasive procedures clearly do not automate traditional surgery. Instead, since their conception, they have provided entirely new, innovative ways of treating patients that were not available before.


Innovation, on this account, involves delivering the outcomes that patients or clients or users have long sought, but using techniques or technology that support radically new underlying processes.


# Elimination
We also find in my story about neurosurgery a clear example of what I call ‘elimination’. The effect of some technologies is not simply to solve a given problem; instead, they cut out the problem in the first place. My favourite case study in eliminative technology is the ‘Great Manure Crisis’ of the 1890s.1 Horses and carriages dominated transport in New York at that time. The horses, in turn, generated unimaginable quantities of ordure; and, moreover, their carcasses littered the streets. Policymakers didn’t know how to cope with what emerged as an unprecedented hazard. Not long after, the motor car was invented and, by the early years of the twentieth century, horses were no longer required to convey people and goods. The new underlying technology, the internal combustion engine, therefore, led to a complete elimination of the poo problem.


So too with AI. In the spirit of preventative medicine, we can easily imagine AI introducing systems and underpinning processes that lead to there being no problem to solve. My focus here is not on artificial intelligence somehow eliminating roles for human beings, although this may happen. My emphasis, rather, is on techniques and technologies that eliminate a whole range of problems to whose resolution many people and organizations are currently devoted.


The eliminative power of technology is rarely noted or discussed, but, in my view, it may end up as the most powerful force of all.


# Examples
This three-way distinction between automation, innovation, and elimination is not watertight. There are overlaps and grey areas. But I hope it offers a wider perspective on the impact of AI than is found in many debates.


Most commentary and popular understanding of AI focuses on automation to the exclusion of innovation and elimination. This is a major error. It means, most significantly, that when people talk about AI and the future of jobs, they are greatly understating or underestimating the likely future impact of AI. There are several widely cited academic and commercial research projects that are open to challenge on this front.2 To believe that threats to jobs will be confined to what has been called ‘task encroachment’,3 that is, to machines taking on some of the tasks that humans undertake today as part of their jobs, is to miss entirely the possibility of innovation (the tasks themselves no longer exist) and of elimination (the problems for which the jobs exist themselves disappear).


## Disputes and Courts
Some further examples should bring this distinction to life. One can be found in the world of law, courts, and dispute resolution. The first sixty-five years or so of dispute-related technology were devoted almost exclusively to automation. Litigators and legal technologists focused on the development and use of technology that streamlined and improved long-standing working practices—the review of documents, presentation of evidence in the courtroom, transcription of evidence, court administration, and legal research for lawyers and judges. None of these systems changed the fundamental nature of litigation. They simply made some of the processes more efficient.


In contrast, in the last decade or so, a scattered community around the world has concentrated instead on what has become known as ‘online dispute resolution’. This is one of the main preoccupations of my book Online Courts and the Future of Justice, in which I ask the question ‘Is court a service or a place?’ I challenge the assumption that to resolve legal disputes, all relevant participants need to assemble in one point in space and time. I and others advocate a variety of online approaches to dispute resolution, including virtual hearings (which became commonplace during Covid); asynchronous service (where there is no hearing or oral evidence—the evidence and arguments are submitted electronically and judges issue their decisions likewise); and even the use of AI to deliver some kind of binding determination. Online dispute resolution is an example of innovation. The outcomes that parties want are delivered using entirely different processes, and the new services simply were not possible before the advent and wide use of digital technology. This is equivalent, in terms of my three-way distinction, to non-invasive therapy in healthcare.


Most court lawyers, when asked about the impact of AI on their work, say that their livelihood is secure because no robot could stand up and plead in court. They are arguing that most of the tasks that make up the activity of oral advocacy could not be taken on by machines. And most researchers would agree and say their jobs are relatively safe. But they thereby ignore the fact that online courts remove the need for oral advocacy. Even if they are right in assuming that no machine could argue in the courtroom (noting that, in the long term, this assumption itself may not be warranted), the point is moot because the set of tasks involved in courtroom appearance is no longer part of the process. Oral advocacy is eliminated. You may be a great blacksmith, but if horses are rarely used, that skill becomes irrelevant.


What about preventative medicine? Well, this too clearly has an analogue in law. I call it ‘dispute avoidance’ rather than dispute resolution—putting a fence at the top of a cliff rather than an ambulance at the bottom. Almost everyone would prefer not to incur the cost and upset of litigation. The best way to achieve this is not to have a legal problem in the first place. AI is now providing useful tools here to help people identify legal risks and difficulties earlier than in the past and also to contain legal problems before they escalate. In the world of disputes, avoiding the pitfall from the outset is another example of elimination—elimination of the very problems that courts exist to solve.


## Cash
Looking beyond medicine and law, many, if not most genuinely game-changing uses of digital technology are examples of innovation and elimination rather than automation. An early illustration here was the ATM or electronic cash dispenser. It wasn’t the case that, prior to their introduction, if people needed money in the middle of the night, they went down to the local bank to find a physical hole carved out in a wall, with some poor soul sitting on the other side, dispensing banknotes on request. It wasn’t that this manual process for distributing cash already existed, but that bankers feared it inefficient (or too chilly), and so decided to automate it. Of course not. Technology enabled and gave rise to a fundamentally new way of delivering the domestic banking service. This innovation, even if not AI-based, transformed domestic banking. Later, credit card technology and electronic payment systems went further and eliminated much of the need for paper cash to be dispensed at all.


## Domestic Chores
One further example. I was invited to participate in a minor way in a research project conducted by the Oxford Internet Institute that was asking AI experts how long it would be before robots would be used for everyday chores in the home.4 One questioner asked, approximately, how long it was felt it would be until a robot could iron clothes. Slightly objectionably, I fear, I said that they were asking the wrong question. A better starting point, I suggested, would be to ask what problem ironing was intended to solve. I was encouraging outcome-thinking rather than process-thinking (see Chapter 3). If we choose to be process-oriented, we might imagine an android wielding a conventional iron. This would be automation and in the spirit of task substitution. If we, instead, opt to be outcome-oriented, we would surely conclude that the end result we wanted is flat fabric. We might be able to imagine a fabric-flattening device that looks nothing like a human or android with an iron but, in the long run, I said to the researchers, the need for any kind of heated mechanism for pressing clothes would disappear—would be eliminated—because materials science (no doubt AI-enabled) would provide us with shirts, trousers, sheets, and so forth that were crease-free. Of course, we are already there to some extent with non-iron, ‘wrinkle-free’ shirts.


## Self-Driving Cars
My last case study in this chapter is built around cars.5 Outcome-thinking invites us to ask what cars are for. Leaving aside the fun of driving, we use cars, as people who have no interest in cars often say, to get from A to B.


Uber and Waze are often noted as fine examples of the power of technology. They are indeed, but both are illustrations of automation—of taxi booking and route planning. One computerizes and, in so doing, streamlines the processes of finding, reserving, and paying for a taxi service. The other optimizes the process by which the quickest route for a journey can be determined, replacing maps or, in London, the renowned ‘knowledge’ of taxi drivers.


In contrast, fully autonomous vehicles are clearly not case studies in automation and task substitution. As suggested in Chapter 4, no one, so far as I know, argues that the best way to develop self-driving cars would be to build robots that could sit in the driving seat of a conventional car and somehow copy a human driver, complete with hands on wheels and feet on pedals. Autonomous vehicles are a staggering innovation (see my first experience, described in Chapter 8). They are driven not by robots but by data. They will fundamentally change the process by which transport outcomes can be achieved. While, at a stretch, it might be said that this is still a form of automation, this is clearly not so for those who are today too young or unable to drive. For them, this technology enables conveyance that was not possible in the past. (I’m in little doubt that our grandchildren will in due course shake their heads in bewilderment, saying, ‘It’s unbelievable that humans used to drive cars.’)


While this technology will mean loss of employment for many, this consequence is not what I mean primarily by ‘elimination’. Of course, as noted, some technologies will, indeed, obviate the need for humans. But the elimination I have in mind with self-driving cars is not of drivers but of the purpose for which vehicles are designed—the problem, so to speak, of getting from A to B. The eliminative technology today in this context is video meetings and, in due course, will be something like AI-generated three-dimensional telepresence in some kind of virtual reality. There will be no need to travel at all.


To conclude this discussion of the three-way distinction of automation, innovation, and elimination, I offer two closing thoughts. First, innovation and elimination create a dependency much greater than automation. If we’ve simply automated an old way of working, then we can generally fall back on the old, analogue, often paper-based, manual system in the event of system crash or failure. In contrast, when we transform (an activity, service, or organization) by innovating or eliminating, we will invariably have introduced entirely new processes or methods, for which there will be no traditional equivalents on which to fall back. Our reliance on innovation and elimination, therefore, comes at a cost. In the event of their failure, unless we have contingency plans in place, we can be left heavily exposed.


Secondly, transformative and eliminative uses of technology often do not rely upon or follow the traditional knowledge and expertise of human experts. They rely, instead, on data, usually in vast quantity. The oft-cited AI-based system that performs impressively in diagnosing malignant melanoma (see Chapter 5) has no represented knowledge of dermatology or histopathology. Rather, it has past images of lesions along with the laboratory results.


In short, to think of AI, as most people do, simply as a tool for automating today’s tasks and activities is enormously to underestimate its current and future impact.
