# Chapter 9
# Harnessing AI
How do we save or at least protect humanity from AI? In keeping with my view of AI as double-edged, I choose the word ‘harnessing’ in this book, where others prefer ‘controlling’ or ‘containing’ AI, because I think ‘harnessing’ reminds us of the extraordinary power and potential of AI but at the same time acknowledges the need to rein it in. Harnessing AI may well be the defining global human endeavour of the second quarter of the twenty-first century.


In this chapter, I explore some aspects of harnessing AI, largely with a view to the long term. I look at the broad challenges, the ethics of AI, the regulation of AI, and I call for an interdisciplinary approach to managing the risks of artificial intelligence. Before I consider these topics, I turn first to a question that I am often asked.


# Why Have We Waited So Long?
We are starting much too late. We were forewarned. Few of the risks laid out in Chapter 8 are new to us. Some of the threats and vulnerabilities have for decades and more sat on the shelves of science fiction. Some were conceived concretely in the middle of the last century by scientists and technologists, most notably and vividly by John von Neumann (in the 1950s: ‘though all [technologies] are intrinsically useful, they can lend themselves to destruction’1), by Norbert Wiener (also in the 1950s: ‘The hour is very late, and the choice of good and evil knocks at our door.’2), and by Joseph Weizenbaum (in the 1970s: ‘science has been gradually converted into a slow-acting poison’3).


So why, I hear you ask, am I so late in raising a red flag? True, in the 1980s, I had assiduously read my von Neumann, Wiener, and Weizenbaum, and I had developed fully operational AI systems. Couldn’t I see forty years ago that the gravest of dangers were looming? Not really. Let me explain.


When I was building and writing on expert systems in the 1980s, this first generation of AI system did presage some thorny ethical and legal issues. We could see that we would want to draw some boundaries in healthcare, for example, identifying those situations where it might be morally wrong to rely on medical diagnostic systems without human oversight. In what circumstances would we want to insist that responsibility must rest with a flesh-and-blood doctor and not a machine? Or in law, it was plain that some challenging legal issues arose from the reliance on systems that purported to offer legal advice. What if loss, damage, or injury followed from a system that made errors in law? And who would be liable? Phillip Capper and I (mainly Phillip, to be honest) wrote at length in 1988 about the legal implications of AI.4 As our systems have become increasingly capable, such problems have persisted and proliferated. This was foreseeable, because we expected technology to advance but—and here is the nub—not at the explosive rate we have recently witnessed.


In common with most people working in AI, we didn’t give serious thought to AI being a clear and imminent threat to humanity itself or a lesser but nonetheless significant threat to our economies or to social cohesion.


In the 1980s, our AI systems were clunky, underpowered, operated in narrow domains, and were viewed with much scepticism by business and government. We could imagine, as fiction writers had, the possibility of much more capable systems, but the threat felt remote. Until the past few years, even the second generation of AI systems didn’t seem to pose immediate threats. Remarkable performances in games, quizzes, medical diagnostics, and arcane subfields of science didn’t look and feel like an existential concern; nor did they obviously augur impending catastrophe.


The performance levels of recent generative AI systems, however, have taken many AI specialists by surprise. Much of the pre-2023 commentary on AI, in both tone and substance, now looks rather dated. What once seemed a mere blink on the distant horizon has now crystallized into high-performance systems. The less technical among us were reassured by AI developers and scientists who told us that AI would be ‘narrow’ and therefore not threatening for the foreseeable future. They failed to anticipate that AI systems would soon outperform us in many tasks and activities. This was a muddled combination of process-thinking and commission of the AI fallacy.


There are some general lessons to be learned here—not to underestimate the pace of technological development, not to dismiss threats too readily, and to hope for the best but prepare for much worse.


# Preparing for Much Worse
How, then, do we manage the risks arising from AI? When I read and hear people—politicians, technologists, commentators—suggesting bullishly that ‘the answer’ is ‘governance’ or ‘regulation’ or ‘guardrails’ or ‘to build ethics in’ or ‘transparency’ or ‘algorithmic audit’ or ‘aligning AI with human values’, I welcome the sentiments but fear that the scale of the challenge is being heavily understated. Likewise, when it is claimed that ‘all we need’ is ‘responsible AI’ or ‘trustworthy AI’ or ‘safe AI’ or ‘secure AI’ or ‘accountable AI,’ I fear that the enormity and complexity of it all is being missed in the soup of buzzwords and catchphrases. I’m sure such proposals are well-meaning, but these reductive slogans run the risk of dangerously minimizing the nature of the endeavour before us. The risks arising from AI vary considerably—in nature, in scale and likelihood of impact, and in possible timeframes. Candidly, the view that one technique or category of technique will do the job and sort out the threats of AI once and for all is naive.


Let’s take a step back. Thinking systematically about the risks of AI requires two lines of thinking. First of all, we have to establish what it is we want and don’t want. Secondly, we then have to find the most effective ways of imposing our preferences.


While the first question is not a technical question, debate and reflection on what we want do need to be grounded in some view of what is technologically conceivable. As I suggest in this chapter, if we are to take a long-term view, if we are wanting sustainable methods of harnessing AI, we must look beyond today’s technologies, and certainly beyond ChatGPT.


My proposal, in outline, is that in assessing our long-term options, we should assume, for the purposes of our discussion, the possibility of developing AI systems that can match or outperform humans in almost all cognitive tasks. I call this ‘what-if-AGI?’ thinking. Instead of taking systems of today and immediately foreseeable technologies as our starting point, the premise I have in mind is that AGI (Artificial General Intelligence) systems are technically feasible. This premise will provoke a richer public debate than focusing, as most do today, on the generative AI of 2024. The question I want us to ask is this—on the assumption of AGI and its associated risks and benefits, what do we want and not want for humanity? In a world with AGI, what social, economic, moral, legal, and other outcomes are we striving towards and which do we want to avoid?


Wrestling with these questions on our behalf and considering which are matters for the state to settle is the province of professional policymakers. I call also for robust public debate, not least on the ethics of AI—that is, on the moral rights and wrongs, the goods and the evils, the acceptable and unacceptable. But we should be in no doubt about the complexities involved here. The intricacies—diplomatic, legal, technical, social, economic, ethical, commercial, security—are profound individually. In combination, they will call for armies of our finest minds across many disciplines, an international effort, with substantial resources allocated and deployed in a small number of years. And that will just be to tackle the risks we can now conceive. Even if we take AGI (human-level performance by machines) as our baseline, new categories of threat will doubtless emerge and new technologies will be developed. The goalposts will always be on the move, although the extent of the movement is not clear and is a matter of contention. This explains the wide range of hypotheses on the future of AI, as laid out in Chapter 2.


The second challenge is finding the best ways of delivering what we want and preventing those outcomes that we deem undesirable. This calls for some kind of ‘regulation’, a term that I use loosely for now to refer to the imposition of binding rules, guidelines, and principles.


We might want to regulate those who design, develop, distribute, and use AI systems. They could be individuals, companies, or countries. To drill down into one level of detail, in regulating the developers, we could impose rules that require the use of certain development standards, methodologies, and tools, alongside required techniques for testing, auditing, validating, and verifying pre-release and operational systems. None of this is new in concept. Engineers working on safety-critical systems have for long been guided and constrained by formal legal and professional standards, while the nuclear industry has been subject to extensive international and national legal control.


One important and relatively new mechanism for enforcing regulation is to embed rules in the AI systems themselves, so they can monitor their own compliance, self-adjust to prevent breach, and automatically block output or activity that is prohibited. This is sometimes referred to as building ‘guardrails’ into AI,5 or reducing law to ‘code’.6


These, then, are the central issues. Where and how should the state intervene? In what regulatory structures should we embody our rules and preferences? Where is there scope for embedding regulation as code?


It’s encouraging that there’s already a great deal of action and activity relating to these matters. Academics and theorists are publishing frenetically. There’s no shortage of thinking and writing. New books on AI ethics are flying onto our shelves.7 To give a sense of the literature on regulation and related topics, for example, The Oxford Handbook of AI Governance, extends to more than 1,000 pages.8 Around the globe, all manner of big players are in a frenzy, working up papers, including the United Nations, the World Bank, the OECD, the Council of Europe, and the European Commission, as well as national governments. Together they have published a deluge of ‘principles’ for the responsible use of AI—many hundreds of guidelines, in my estimation.9 The great thing about AI principles is that there are so many to choose from.


In fact, to cope with this ‘principle proliferation,’10 a sub-industry has emerged, of authors and institutes engaged in meta-analysis of the many sets of principles, seeking to distil these down into a handful of common principles.11 Impressive though much of this work is, I find that many of the principles laid down fall into the category of ‘motherhood and apple pie’, they are often too general and vague, and there is rarely guidance on how to resolve conflicts between the principles themselves, because none seems to be overriding. I can’t disagree, for example, that ‘fairness’ or ‘human values’ or ‘autonomy’ are important, but I have found these concepts difficult to apply in practice in my advisory work. More, I don’t think that the existing principles help much with my Risk Categories 1, 2, 3, and 4 (Existential Risks, Risks of Catastrophe, Political Risks, and Socio-Economic Risks). I also doubt that they would find universal favour in different cultures and political systems, a point to which I return shortly.


In sum, as with AI generally, then also with ethics and regulations, we are still at the foothills, and even here, there’s more heat than light. A natural instinct is to suppose or hope that we already have all the tools to tackle the full suite of AI risks. All we need to do, surely, is to build ethics in, draw up some regulations, and insert some guardrails into the systems themselves. Job done. Or so it is often said. In contrast, I believe we need new tools, techniques, and concepts, indeed an entirely different infrastructure for thinking about and meeting the full set of challenges.


With this in mind, let’s put ethics and regulations under the microscope.


# On AI Ethics
One key dimension of what we want and don’t want from AI is the moral dimension. There’s a tendency among many commentators and policymakers, but much less among academics, to insist that our focus today should be on the current technology (generative AI) and so on the short-term risks. I fear this leads to rather limited thinking about the ethics of AI, especially in the worlds of business and politics. There are two related problems to unpack here. The first is that our AI debates over what is morally right or wrong tend to be constrained by what is technologically possible today. The most difficult ethical issues of tomorrow are often thereby dodged because the focus is on current technologies rather than impending and not yet invented technologies whose greater capabilities will raise much more challenging questions. Most current views and arguments on the ethics of AI are, therefore, incomplete, fragile even, and certainly subject to review as new systems emerge. This is not an error in itself, but it does stand in the way of a widespread aspiration to establish a set of enduring principles or, if not, a clear articulation of the range of ethical challenges.


A second and closely related issue is that many discussions about AI are hampered by disagreement over what machines can and cannot do, full stop. Again and again, commentators and AI specialists have excluded topics from ethical discourse on the ground that machines will never be able to do x, y, or z. The field of AI is littered with nevers. And yet, as new techniques have been developed, these exclusions have left gaps in our ethical debate.


These two problems are closely allied to the idea of technological myopia, as discussed in Chapter 2. This is the error of evaluating the promise of future technologies by reference to today’s limitations. It is to fail to accommodate the more or less inevitable development of new techniques—the not yet invented technologies. In relation to the ethics of AI, it is a similar failure, prevalent in the private and public sectors, and leads to tactical and transient ethical thinking which is anathema to robust long-term planning and policymaking.


To overcome this short-termism in AI ethics, I recommend that we explicitly carve out space for a more ambitious and confronting public debate. As mentioned earlier, I call this ‘what-if-AGI?’ thinking. This is my proposed thought experiment that invites us to assume we will develop systems that can rival or outperform humans in almost all cognitive tasks. It then requires us to think boldly and imaginatively about the impact and implications of such systems.


To engage in ‘what-if-AGI?’ thinking in AI ethics is to ask what moral limits or constraints we would impose on AGI systems. Ethically, where would we draw our red lines? How would we wish to share the planet with these massively capable systems? Do we find the idea abhorrent? Does it offend our autonomy, our dignity, our humanity, our sense of our place in the scheme of things?12


If we challenge ourselves by thinking in this way—and this is the point of ‘what-if-AGI?’ thinking—we can really stretch and stress-test our views and instincts about AI and our future without being inhibited by putative technological limitations. In so doing, we will also extend ourselves beyond AI ethics of today, which is largely the ethics of automation—that is, the ethics of systematizing and computerizing tasks and activities already undertaken by humans.


‘What-if-AI?’ thinking invites us to consider the ethics of tasks and activities that only machines can take on. This is largely unchartered territory. Focusing on machines behaving ‘like the best of us’, as Nigel Shadbolt and Roger Hampson do in their book, As If Human, does not take us far enough.13 Assuming they perform at much higher levels than humans, we will have a new set of issues to consider. For example, we will need to debate how we treat massively capable systems and discuss what level of respect they will deserve. According to the philosopher, A. C. Grayling, this is a ‘little-discussed arena of difficulty’.14 Shadbolt and Hampson start their book with the observation that ‘If an AI spontaneously combusts, that’s an expensive nuisance. If a human being spontaneously combusts, that’s a tragedy.’15 I’m not sure I agree. Even if, for now, we ascribe no consciousness or will to our robots, I find it entirely conceivable that we might become both attached to and reliant on them. We might be traumatized by their combustion; even more so, if they exhibit AGI or near-AGI. Others might disagree. But this is the kind of conversation we must now entertain.


To answer all the questions thrown up by ‘what-if-AGI?’ thinking, we should engage our best philosophers. More, we must draw on centuries of our finest philosophical thinking. We need to be guided by Plato, Aristotle, and Kant rather than—with respect—Sam Altman, Elon Musk, and Mark Zuckerberg.


We need to rise above the homespun philosophizing that is popular in the tech community. Platitude is not principle. Visceral moralizing is not coherent ethical discourse. We must lift the debate too beyond the well-meaning insistence by countless lawyers, social scientists, and health professionals who are rightly concerned about the impact of AI on society but wrong to think that the answer is a straightforward job of slotting human values into our AI systems. Is it, in pursuit of human values, for example, to prohibit or restrict the deployment of AI to the billions of people who are currently deprived of decent healthcare, access to justice, and education, because some of the systems are currently deemed to be high-risk?


There are echoes in today’s nascent work on AI ethics of the field of medical ethics in the 1970s. At that time, moral decisions relating to health were largely in the hands of senior physicians and surgeons. It fell to these expert professionals, on a daily basis, to stipulate what was or wasn’t morally acceptable. There was little systematic thinking in the background about medical ethics, little training of doctors on ethical decision-making, and little public discussion of the many moral dilemmas which were being addressed on the hoof. There were rules and superiors to follow—ethics by diktat.


This state of affairs was challenged by a number of moral philosophers (one of whom was my mentor, Robin Downie, so I saw some of this debate close up16). They argued that ethical issues in medicine should not be settled simply by the ad hoc pronouncements of well-intentioned physicians or surgeons who happened to be good eggs. Others had an interest in life and death, in pain and health. Expertise was needed that went well beyond the experience of most workers in the caring professions. The result was the birth of a flourishing discipline, medical ethics, an interdisciplinary field that has exerted great positive influence on healthcare.


Likewise, the field of AI ethics needs to mature and do so rapidly. There is too much at stake to leave the field in the hands of technologists and people who feel strongly, no matter how good their intentions might be. Some scholars have specified in considerable detail the credentials required of an AI ethicist.17 This will be a sobering read for some who might feel they are sufficiently qualified. But it’s a salutary appeal both for humility and for genuine expertise.


I call again for philosophically informed public conversation about the impact of AI like the one held in the UK in the early 1980s over the moral implications of emerging techniques such as in vitro fertilization and test-tube babies.18 A national inquiry and consultation was led by Mary Warnock, an eminent philosopher. This generated excellent debate among scientists, scholars, journalists, and the public, and significantly elevated the level of general understanding of the main issues which were clarified, if not fully resolved at the time. We urgently need a similar scale of debate on the ethical limitations that we should impose on AI. I recommend that this be based on ‘what-if-AGI?’ thinking. In turn, we will need to allow ourselves some leaps of imagination—in expectation of the impact of not yet invented systems, and looking beyond automation to innovation and elimination.


One more thing. It is to fly in the face of endless, respectable philosophy (metaethics, to be precise) to assume without argument, as commentators and AI specialists often do, that somehow there is some single conception of the right and good that we can plug into our AI systems as some kind of Lego®-like ethics module, or that there is indeed a single coherent set of values with which our systems should ‘align’.19 There may well be some broad consensus in some parts of the world over some of the ethical issues raised by AI (see my earlier discussion of AI principles), but as Aristotle taught us, and I paraphrase, fire burns in both Greece and Persia but people’s idea of right and wrong varies from place to place.20 In other words, it won’t be enough for Silicon Valley technologists to think, as I have heard some say, that they will simply need to get their heads together and make sure they all use AI responsibly. Their conception of responsible and ethical AI is unlikely to be the same as the thinking and practice of, say, policymakers in China, North Korea, and Russia. Are there ways of determining whose ethical position is right or wrong? Philosophers have been divided on this very issue for thousands of years, and objectivists, subjectivists, and relativists have very different views on whether or how some moral disagreements can be settled.21 These differences seem to be lost on many ‘experts’ on AI ethics.


# On Law and Regulation
Much of the current debate on the law of AI is devoted to analysis of what the current law is. For example, many legal practitioners and academics are exploring the copyright implications, in different jurisdictions, of scraping the web for the training data needed for large language models. A second focus of legal debate is on what the law ought to be—that is, on what new rules are needed and in what form.


Existing AI legislation and regulation already varies around the world. According to UNESCO’s research, since 2016 more than thirty countries have passed laws that explicitly mention AI. They have identified nine different approaches to regulation, some more interventionist than others.22


The American light touch is heavily shaped by the market, which effectively means by the big tech lobby. Major AI businesses there resist heavy intrusion by government. The Chinese approach so far is more obviously state-driven. The focus in the EU, embodied in the AI Act (2024), is on the protection of the many rights on which artificial intelligence is thought to impinge.


It’s not my intention here to summarize the substantive law and regulations on AI that have been introduced. Others have done this creditably.23 Instead, I want to draw attention to four worries that I harbour about the general approach to regulation and legislation that’s being taken around the world. In discussing these, I am more concerned with what the law ought to be.


My first concern is that rule-makers are regulating too narrowly. There are two aspects to this. On the one hand, many, if not most politicians and policymakers are focusing on and seeking to regulate only one type of AI, namely generative AI. This is short-sighted. Recent advances in generative AI are wholly remarkable, but they are simply the latest breakthroughs and will assuredly not be the last. To seek to regulate generative AI alone is to fall foul of another form of technological myopia. It is to ignore the increasing capability of our systems as well as the not yet invented technologies.


One clear practical issue is that we simply cannot roll out our entire policymaking and legislative apparatus every time there is a major new advance in AI. As the new developments come at us at an ever-increasing pace, we will have neither the time nor the capacity.


To base the regulatory scope on generative AI or other techniques is to pursue an unhelpful form of process-thinking. It is to imagine that what needs regulating is existing enabling technologies. It is to place too great an emphasis on how AI systems work rather than the impact they might have. The EU seemed to struggle with this issue. Not long before their AI Act was apparently ready for implementation, the arrival of ChatGPT in late 2022 sent them back to the drawing board. Policymakers had previously said the new rules would apply generically, but in fact they seemed unable to cope even with the first wave of generative AI systems. The final version of the AI Act remains open to a similar challenge. AI systems that were trained using specified levels of computing power (more than 1025floating operations per second (FLOPs)) are stipulated to be high risk.24 In contrast, the Council of Europe’s international treaty on AI, opened for signature in early September 2024, is said to be ‘technology-neutral’ so that it can ‘stand the test of time’.25


In broad terms, the best approach I believe is for legislation and regulations to focus on addressing the general harms that might arise from the development and deployment of massively capable systems rather than trying to focus on existing types of AI. If the regulation is indeed to have significant shelf life, I suggest it not be framed in terms of highly specific rules. It should be focused on prevention or control of undesirable outcomes rather than on particular technologies.


The other way that rule-makers are regulating too narrowly around the world is by focusing, by and large, on only two of my seven categories of risk, namely risks of unreliability and risks of reliance. My strong plea is that, in the spirit of ‘what-if-AGI?’ thinking, we work much harder—starting yesterday—on developing the policy and legal instruments to help manage existential risks and risks of catastrophe (a matter, generally, for international treaties and other arrangements) as well as political risks, socio-economic risks, and the risks of inaction (a matter more for the local law of nation states). Again, putting our minds to how we might regulate for AGI will call for a leap in imagination—in anticipating the impact of not yet invented systems and in thinking beyond automation.


Whereas my first worry is that rule-makers are regulating too narrowly, my second worry, paradoxically, is that some rule-makers are also regulating too broadly. Well-intentioned politicians, for example, who are anxious to prevent harms arising from AI systems run the risk of throwing the baby out with the bathwater. Instinctively, it might feel right, as many policymakers believe, to impose a massive compliance burden on the developers and providers of AI systems that are considered to be high risk. However, in AI, as elsewhere, the best can be the enemy of the good, or indeed of the better. I am told frequently by lawyers and doctors that if there is no human-expert involvement, there are severe risks posed by systems that offer legal or medical advice on which lay people might rely. Of course, I can see the risks here, but I am conscious of the much greater global social tragedy that most people on planet earth cannot afford legal or medical help at all.


The test, therefore, is not an objective one about risk, as many policymakers and commentators seem to believe. At least in some circumstances, it’s a messier relative test about whether having an AI system is better than having nothing at all. On some utilitarian calculus, it’s about, overall, whether having particular categories of AI system would be better than no systems, about when good enough is good enough, even if not as good as the best or great humans. The test should not be one of perfection; it should be about improvement. Whether an AI system is high-risk, therefore, is a necessary question to ask. The altogether less tidy further question is whether introducing a system would represent an advance on the status quo (which is not to say that any change is always better than nothing). We should also be mindful that we don’t expect more from our AI systems than we get from humans. We may allow that to err is human, but are nervous about extending the same tolerance to our machines. My point is that in regulating too enthusiastically, we might lose some of the undoubted benefits that AI can afford.


My third concern is about the rather loose use of the term ‘regulation’, because this can lead to superficiality. Others talk of ‘governance’ in the same fashion. So far in this book, I am rather guilty of this sloppiness myself, in using ‘regulation’ to refer to a multitude of normative constraints. But when we roll up our sleeves and reduce our preferred norms to some kind of binding instrument, lawyers know that we have a rich range of principles, rules, and guidance at our disposal, and that the selection of one technique rather than another requires deep expertise. Indeed, the question of when to introduce new law and when to leave an issue to be settled by the market or self-regulation or codes of practice or social standards is also one of some subtlety. As is, for example, in the UK, the question of whether a new set of rules or principles should be introduced through primary or delegated legislation. And so, I am simply raising a flag and saying that while ‘regulation’ is a useful umbrella term to refer to some kind of authoritative body of rules which are backed by the coercive powers of the state, those who speak about regulation should be alive to the complexities.


The three concerns just noted coalesce into a fourth—I call this the ‘rush to regulate’. The current response to AI is too reactive, too short-term, and being conducted too hastily. I understand the pressure on politicians to respond emphatically to, say, AI-based online harms to children and egregious invasions of privacy. But we have not yet had serious public debate about these and other issues. Our thinking is fuzzy and ill-formed. Less diplomatically, we are in danger of too hastily churning out populist, knee-jerk, half-baked restrictions on the AI technology of the day. This could be counterproductive—not, in fact, preventing harms, while simultaneously missing some golden opportunities.


# Upgrading Our Justice System
The concerns just discussed relate to how we are currently coping with the regulatory challenges (or not). If we turn to the longer term, a more fundamental issue rears its head. To what extent are our justice systems themselves—our lawmaking processes, our court systems, our basic legal concepts—well suited to the era of massively capable systems? In the rush to regulate for the short term, this question of the future adequacy of our long-standing legal institutions has drawn scant attention.


This is my home turf. Back in 1996, I wrote a book called The Future of Law. I explained there that twentieth-century legal processes and legal services had evolved to function in a print-based industrial society and I predicted that our legal system would creak and worse as we moved into what I would now call an AI-based digital society.26 And so it has come to pass.


The tendency of many lawmakers, judges, and lawyers, however, is to believe and argue that new challenges can be handled by traditional legal concepts and institutions. In England, for example, they will speak of the endurance and flexibility of the common law system under which the law can be developed imaginatively by responsive judges. They find it hard to imagine that the system of which they are an integral part may not endure. This, in part, is a form of not-us thinking.


Where does the evidence point us? Take lawmaking first. For more than fifty years, specialist technology lawyers have complained that legislation, regulation, and judicial decisions tend to lag many years behind new technologies—a five–ten-year lag is commonly noted. By the time officials and politicians have assessed any given emerging technology, come to a view, and drafted, scrutinized, and redrafted proposed law, the world has moved on, if not changed comprehensively. The explosive advance of AI will exacerbate this. Our rule-makers will struggle to keep up unless they work differently. Similarly with judge-made or judge-developed law—complex cases take years to resolve. We must find ways of accelerating our processes for creating, modifying, repealing, and developing the law. The five–ten-year lag will not do.


As the capabilities of our systems advance at a ferocious and, it is likely, accelerating rate, we will require legislative processes that can generate laws and regulations in weeks rather than years. Here AI itself will come to the rescue. I envisage real-time monitoring of the impact of existing legislation, and sentiment analysis to determine the mood of the people, and so society’s appetite for change—and the market’s too. AI systems will themselves draft legal rules in natural language and in executable code—that is, in the form of computer programs. This will provide us with powerful new debugging tools for stress-testing draft legislation—for example, for conducting what-if analyses or for identifying inconsistencies, syntactic ambiguities, and gaps in rules. Executable versions of legal rules will also be directly convertible into straightforward question-and-answer systems which will help everyone to understand their legal rights and duties. More, AI-generated code will routinely be implanted into physical systems such as self-driving cars and intelligent buildings, which will enable rules and legislation to self-adjust dynamically under given conditions. This will mark a new age in the ‘internet of things’. Compliance will be assured not by having more lawyers in the loop but because computer code will bake the law into everyday objects and activities. In the future, the use of these and not yet invented AI tools for drafting and implementing legislation should come to be required by the rule of law, a concept whose reach should extend as AI advances.


What about our courts? In England, formal litigation has not changed much since the late nineteenth century, and so it should flabbergast no one that our dispute resolution systems might need more than a mild tweak to cope with an entirely different pace of society and with types of AI disputes that were inconceivable to those who drafted the Judicature Acts of 1873 and 1875.


As a generality, court systems around the world (I am speaking of civil, that is, not criminal disputes) cost too much, take too long, are too combative, and are unintelligible to all but lawyers. In the AI era, if the law is to remain relevant and command respect, we will need much more accessible, affordable, and rapid processes for resolving disputes as well for developing the law. This is the guiding philosophy of the ODR (online dispute resolution) community of which I am a part. In my book of 2019, Online Courts and the Future of Justice, I show how we should use technology to resolve disputes in the twenty-first century.27 We already have the technology.


I also believe we’ll need to rethink some of our fundamental legal concepts. For example, while many lawyers endlessly debate the current copyright implications of generative AI, I believe we need to reconceptualize our intellectual property law entirely. This is a regime whose domain is ideas and creations expressed as words and images, and yet its foundations were laid long before computers were invented. I side with John Perry Barlow’s claim, made in the early days of the worldwide web, that ‘Intellectual property law cannot be patched, retrofitted, or expanded to contain digitized expression.’28


Similarly, the concept of legal personality needs to be revisited. It will not do to think, as some commentators have suggested, that somehow we can pigeonhole massively capable AI systems into the same slots as pets or corporations for legal purposes. To use the terminology of the historian Yuval Noah Harari, these systems will be more like high-performing ‘aliens’29 with whom it is likely we will be sharing the planet. Accordingly, in my view, we will need to contemplate entirely new forms of legal personality if we are to endow AI systems with appropriate entitlements, impose sensible duties on them, and grant them fitting permissions.30


Likewise, we should revisit how we allocate responsibility and liability when AI systems lead to blameless humans suffering loss, damage, or injury. We shouldn’t carry forward our anomalous structures because they often render it a matter of luck whether an innocent person is compensated (it’s often down to whether a wrongdoer has funds or is insured). Instead, I advise that we establish public civil compensation schemes for AI loss, funded by the providers of high-impact AI. These would immediately recompense those who have suffered loss and would leave it to the various AI providers to allocate liability among themselves.


Even the law of contract may need to be overhauled. Already we see it creaking—when we are invited daily, for example, to click to confirm that we have read many thousands of lines of legalese. In an AI society, tomorrow’s autonomous, massively capable systems will somehow have to have arrangements for collaborating and trading with one another. We may well need to find other ways of providing the certainty, predictability, and means of enforcement that the law of contract offers today. More, as AI systems transcend the boundaries set by international law, we may need to reconsider how nation states, their citizens, and their machines interact with and create reciprocal expectations of one another on a global basis. The legal historian, Sir Henry Maine, argued in the nineteenth century that a human’s obligations in early societies arose from their status at birth, whereas in later societies they arose from contract. One wonders whether there will be a further movement ‘from Status to Contract’ to…I know not what.31


Work should start immediately on upgrading our legal and justice systems—on overhauling our legislative processes, on introducing online dispute resolution, and on reconceptualizing our tired legal tools. We cannot continue to stretch twenty-first-century legal problems on our nineteenth-century Procrustean bed. We should deploy ‘what-if-AGI?’ thinking here too.


Radical though my proposed upgrades to our justice systems may appear, they may not yet be sufficient to quell the ‘unaccountable power’ of AI and big tech providers—we might need to go much further and embrace a new political system to maintain our freedom and democracy in the AI age.32


# A Multidisciplinary Approach
As we navigate into the future, largely sightless, I worry that too much is being asked of our technologists. We expect them, of course, to build systems and build businesses. And the best of them do so superbly. But we often also invite them to cast light on the future. Here, as a generality, their track record is middling. The literature is littered with false prophecies from the technology community, along with some dogmatic but misguided dismissals of future possibilities.


Troublesome too for non-specialists is how often AI experts themselves are divided, not least over whether, for example, AI is going to fizzle out, plateau, or burgeon, or whether we are on the road to Armageddon or salvation.


We all have our favourite examples of digital clangers. I’ve always enjoyed the predictions of Thomas J. Watson, the chair of IBM, who in 1943 said, ‘I think there is a world market for maybe five computers’; and in 1977, of Ken Olson, the president, chair, and founder of the then computer giant Digital Equipment Corp., who proclaimed that ‘There is no reason that anyone would want a computer in their home.’33


Less dramatically, in the world of AI, most experts working on rule-based expert systems in the 1980s—including me—completely failed to foresee the impact of machine learning. People working on the early machine learning systems in the first fifteen years or so of the twenty-first century did not expect the recent leap that we now call generative AI. Many AI specialists were insisting until ChatGPT came along that, for the foreseeable future, AI would only be capable of taking on ‘narrow’ tasks. These claims were often made to offer reassurance that most jobs would be safe and that fears of existential risk were unwarranted. This was incorrect. It was clear to many of us that many so-called narrow systems, if run together, could take on broader tasks. Nor was it a huge leap to think that much more capable systems were likely to be invented, as has proven to be the case. (When I recently asked a professor of computer science about this mistake, he said, ‘Yes, I’ve taken the “narrow slide” out of my student deck.’)


Computer scientists and technologists are generally sound when asked to extrapolate from what we have today. But they regularly miss the cumulative effect of numerous innovations. More, they have no foresight of revolutionary techniques in laboratories and businesses of which they have no visibility. Worse, they tend to confuse the limits of their own approach to AI with limits to AI generally; they fail to recognize and even downplay the possibility of fundamental breakthroughs or paradigm shifts. Technologically, intellectually, emotionally, and often economically, many technologists are committed to the current wave of AI systems.


Beyond expecting them to be soothsayers, technology entrepreneurs and computer scientists are also being invited to guide us on the ethical and regulatory implications of new and emerging systems. This can be deeply problematic. There’s no reason that the inventor of a new technique or someone who has successfully commercialized a new technology has any greater insight into the morality or the governance of their creation than the average person. To extend my earlier analogy, this is like believing doctors are experts in medical ethics and the regulation of medicine. Or that the hangman was an authority on the morality and law of capital punishment.


Crucially, technologists are also conflicted. They have clear vested interests, whether as commercial players or as thought leaders. They have deep interest, conscious or unconscious, in promoting or discouraging certain classes and particular versions of systems and uses.


Nonetheless, many of these technology experts seem more than willing to hold forth expansively on ethics and regulation, advancing their views before government committees and at major gatherings. Some write papers, laying out their views on the philosophy, politics, and economics of emerging technologies. Often—there is no easy way to say this—these are not fully informed. Some humility would help. As experts themselves, they should recognize the need for deep, nuanced, expert thinking. One danger here is that some of the tech people have massive followings and their ideas are fastened upon and accepted as canonical.


Technology is too important to be left to technologists. I do not think, by default, we should allow AI engineers to become unaccountable ‘social engineers’.34


As massively capable systems are developed and new advances are made frequently, grappling with the evolution of our creation is an onerous responsibility for humans. How, then, do we address the defining challenge for humanity in the twenty-first century?


During the 1990s, a growing body of academics began to take a systematic interest in the social and ethical impacts of the internet and the World Wide Web. At the University of Oxford this interest led to the launch in 2001 of the Oxford Internet Institute, the brainchild of Andrew Graham, the then Master of Balliol College, with the considerable philanthropic support of Dame Stephanie Shirley.35 I was one of the founder members of the advisory board and well remember our early discussions about naming the institute. Would there be an internet in twenty years? If so, would this be what we might call it? Blessedly, we decided not to use the term ‘information superhighway’, which was current then. At the time, there were 500 million users of the internet. Now there are over five billion.


In retrospect we seemed naive. We hadn’t witnessed the birth of Facebook (2004), YouTube (2005), Twitter (2006), Instagram (2010), or indeed Apple’s iPhone (2007). It was early days. But we shared a conviction that online life would be very different from pre-internet life and that this emerging phenomenon required deep scrutiny and assessment.


Our vision for the Institute was to build a centre devoted to studying and teaching the impact of the internet on society. It would not be a home for computer scientists but instead an assembly of sociologists, economists, ethicists, lawyers, psychologists, policymakers, and others dedicated to understanding, explaining, and predicting this new online world—studying the internet and related systems and services in an interdisciplinary spirit. We felt that the internet was too important to be left to the dotcom entrepreneurs and technologists. The Institute has been a great success, demonstrating above all that we work best on big problems when we venture out of our pigeonholes and collaborate across fields.


I submit that AI should be tackled in a similar way the world over, not just in the groves of academe, but in industry, government, and diplomacy too. In the same interdisciplinary spirit, business, policy, and scholarly thinkers, with voices of equal volume, should convene with technologists to work through how to save humanity from and with AI.


The complexities involved will call for armies of our finest minds, an international effort with great resources allocated and deployed in a small number of years. In scale, this is our generation’s Apollo programme.
