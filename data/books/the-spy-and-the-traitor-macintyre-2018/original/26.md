## 17. Afterword
Following the publication of The Spy and the Traitor in September 2018, I received numerous letters, emails and telephone calls from former officers of the three intelligence services directly involved in this story – MI6, the CIA and the KGB – who had read the book, and wished to pass comment.   Many of their responses were complimentary and corroborative; some were not; the core elements of the story were undisputed, but several took issue with my interpretations and conclusions, as well as certain details of spycraft.  Oleg Gordievsky remains a controversial figure, not least for exposing the contacts between the Labour leader Michael Foot and the KGB.  More than three decades after the events described in this book, British, American and Russian perspectives on the case remain very different, and in some respects diametrically opposed.  For some Russians, particularly those who regret the break-up of the Soviet Union, to which he contributed, Gordievsky remains a hate-figure.  Gordievsky’s actions, motivations, impact and legacy continue to provoke disagreement between the former combatants of the Cold War: that is further proof of his enduring and unique significance.


Some of these former intelligence officers are identified by name below, but most must remain anonymous.  I have presented their different viewpoints in the form of a discussion, addressing some key aspects of the case.


Perhaps the most savoury bone of contention in the post-publication furore was the question of when, whether and how Aldrich Ames betrayed Gordievsky. The CIA maintains that Ames probably did not identify Gordievsky as a mole in May 1985 before he was summoned back to Moscow.  One CIA source cited a joint CIA/FBI damage report, written after Ames himself was arrested, which ‘accepted Ames’s account that he delivered the damaging information in June’ – by which time Gordievsky was already back in Moscow and under close KGB surveillance.  Most MI6 sources challenge that version: ‘The report concluded that they could not be sure Ames passed on the information about Oleg Gordievsky in May, though he certainly did so in June.’  But none of the CIA officers I communicated with offered an alternative explanation.  The idea that Ames might not have been the only betrayer is easier for the CIA to swallow, since it suggests that the initial leak might have come from Britain, but most of those on the British side believe, as I do, that there is no longer a mystery: Ames betrayed Gordievsky to the KGB.  One MI6 officer closely involved in the case believes that the KGB investigators may have obtained corroborative evidence as a result of the trial of Arne Treholt, the Norwegian spy: that trial took place in camera, but evidence that Gordievsky had revealed Treholt’s links to the KGB might have been secretly leaked to the Soviet embassy and passed on to Moscow.   Intriguingly, none of the former KGB officers who contacted me denied that Ames had been the source, or sought to propagate the notion that the KGB had already rumbled Gordievsky through other sources.  One former KGB officer argued that Ames deserves credit for having identified Gordievsky as the mole.  Mikhail Lyubimov writes: ‘I don’t know exactly what Ames reported, but clearly there was not enough evidence for his immediate arrest.’  That would seem to support my own view, shared by the majority of those involved in the case, that during his first meeting with the KGB in Washington Ames provided sufficient information to prompt the investigation by Viktor Budanov and trigger Gordievsky’s recall.


On details of tradecraft, one CIA source questioned whether Gordievsky’s meeting with Kaplan in Copenhagen (p. 50) took place as I described it, and it was described to me: ‘Why would a service set up a meeting with the target (who could be identified by certain passers-by) in a window?’ MI6, however, maintains that, had the KGB been watching, a secretive rendezvous would have seemed suspicious, whereas meeting openly in a café window would indicate that these were simply two old friends innocently reuniting.  ‘Mike Stokes would have found it useful to have sight of the meeting,’ said one former MI6 officer.


The issue of codenames also prompted debate.  The CIA denied that MI6 was codenamed UPTIGHT.  ‘It does not fit the pattern of cryptonyms for UK elements,’ said one senior officer.  One British officer, however, recalled that the CIA station in London once accidentally included the reference to MI6 as UPTIGHT, and awarded him a ‘personal UPTIGHT number’.  ‘I returned the copy of the letter and would sometimes rib them about being uptight.  But it is possible that it was a term invented by CIA London Station and not necessarily registered or approved by Langley.’  Both MI6 and the CIA insisted that, since the war, all codewords were chosen from a list of deliberately meaningless terms, but, as one officer remarked, ‘one could reject a pseudonym as being inappropriate and ask for another one with a more melodious twang.’  Some in MI6 disliked the codename NOCTON, since it sounded like ‘knocked on’, an infringement in rugby; others thought HETMAN sounded too close to ‘hitman’ for comfort.  ‘The use of OVATION was quite clearly bombastic in tone,’ said one officer, ‘it had been seen as self-congratulatory and annoyed many Whitehall mandarins.’


I had expected to be either denounced as a propagandist in Russia, or ignored.  In fact, the responses of former KGB officers were remarkably gracious.  ‘The Gordievsky case was a tremendous success,’ wrote Maksim Parshikov.  ‘Though one might argue they were lucky to have a volunteer walk in, unlike the Cambridge Five, who needed much more effort to spot, cultivate and recruit.’   Despite being personally betrayed by his old friend, Lyubimov paid tribute to Gordievsky’s raw courage, as well as the daring of Operation PIMLICO.  ‘To offer an agent such an escape plan from the clutches of the KGB was tantamount to an invitation to be executed … the more straightforward and “humane” Americans prefer to offer their spies deadly poison in case of failure.’  Both Parshikov and Lyubimov were nettled by Gordievsky’s depiction of the Soviet Union as a cultural wasteland.  ‘To the best of my memory, you could hear classical music all over the country,’ wrote Parshikov.  Lyubimov quoted a letter he had received from Gordievsky during his KGB posting in London, suggesting that he was not overly impressed with the cultural offerings in Britain: ‘I don’t see those charms that you talk about – theatres, museums, etc.  My only solace is Radio Three, which broadcasts only serious music. I listen to it constantly.’  Lyubimov’s affection for Britain is undimmed: ‘I adore cats, English literature from Shakespeare to Lewis Carroll to le Carré, whisky, especially Glenlivet, Winnie the Pooh, Henley Regatta, horse  racing at Ascot, London parks, Covent Garden and the Tate Gallery.  I even like British intelligence in the shape of Kim Philby and George Blake.  Isn’t it great to drown in cigar smoke in a tweed jacket and flannel trousers?’


The revelations about the extent and profitability of Michael Foot’s KGB contacts set off a brief but fierce firestorm in the British media.  Foot’s supporters, including Labour’s leader, Jeremy Corbyn, claimed that a revered figure of the left  had been traduced when he could no longer defend himself.  Some on the right insisted that Foot’s actions were tantamount to treachery.   It was not clear that either side had taken the trouble to read what I had written. The truth, of course, lies somewhere between the extremes: Foot was neither a traitor nor an innocent; he was an extremely clever man, whose long and lucrative dalliance with the KGB was exceptionally foolish.


Unsurprisingly, some Russians doubt the purity of Gordievsky’s motives in spying for the UK.  Lyubimov wrote: ‘I am sure that the main factor that pushed Gordievsky into the hands of MI6 was vanity.  I venture to suggest that he had an unquenchable thirst to appear in the Western world not just as an ordinary defector, but as an anti-Soviet Messiah.’   Parshikov also points out that while many fellow KGB officers were sceptical of the ‘sclerotic’ Kremlin leadership, only Gordievsky chose to work for an enemy power in the way he did: ‘Though he justified his actions as a fight against Communism and the Soviet Union, did Oleg really hate his Motherland, rather than communism?’


Oleg’s former KGB colleagues also question the impact of the intelligence he passed to MI6.   ‘He could not surrender any serious agents either in Copenhagen or London because, alas, at that time we sat “with bare bottoms” and had no agents,’ wrote Lyubimov. ‘Gordievsky could not know what was going on in the politburo, the highest authority.’  The Russians have, however, alerted me to one Soviet spy allegedly exposed by Gordievsky of whom I was previously unaware: Alexei Kozlov, a Soviet intelligence officer arrested in South Africa in 1981, who was later swapped for eight unidentified Westerners.  Lyubimov wrote that Kozlov, a former classmate of Oleg’s at the Moscow State Institute of International Relations, was tortured in a South African prison; Parshikov claimed he was ‘crippled’.


‘It is clear that in 1983 Oleg “saved the world” by revealing the Soviet thinking behind RYAN,’ conceded Parshikov. ‘But I wonder if Oleg has achieved his life goal?   OK, the USSR doesn’t exist and we in Russia have already forgotten about Communism.  But has it become better, safer, for the world?’


At a time of escalating tension between Russia and the West, particularly in the realm of espionage, the world is entering another period of insecurity and mutual distrust, which some have called a new Cold War.  That is why Russia and the West do not see Gordievsky in the same light, and never will: a brilliant spy in Western eyes, and a rank traitor in Russian ones.


Of all the responses to the book, that from its subject was both the most gratifying and the briefest.  Oleg Gordievsky did not read the book before publication; he then read it twice, and sent his assessment in a single line, written in a wobbly hand: ‘It is flawless.’  This book is certainly not flawless, but if it has helped to bring to the world’s attention a remarkable, brave and complex man, and an important episode in recent history, then it will have served its purpose.
